But the main story dominating the national conversation today is a massive espionage scandal that has deeply rattled the highest levels of the state. Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani has officially expelled two military attachés from the Russian Embassy in Rome. The expulsion follows the shocking arrest of two former Italian intelligence officers who are accused of selling highly classified NATO secrets, European rearmament plans, and even sensitive Vatican data to Moscow for nearly two decades. Intercepted wiretaps reveal the rogue agents handed over thousands of files to Russian military intelligence, often in exchange for cash bribes as low as 4,000 euros. 

The media framing of this intelligence disaster presents two very different realities. The progressive press, led by La Repubblica and La Stampa, focuses on the humiliating vulnerability of Italy's national security apparatus. They highlight how easily NATO’s defense strategies—including details on long-range missiles for Ukraine—were compromised right under the government's nose, painting a picture of an intelligence community riddled with systemic blind spots. On the other side, the conservative establishment is spinning the fallout as a decisive victory. Il Giornale heavily emphasizes the government's swift and unyielding response, framing Tajani’s expulsion of the Russian diplomats as a bold defense of Italian sovereignty. The inescapable takeaway is that the Meloni government, already bruised by its recent public friction with the Trump administration over NATO spending, is using this scandal to aggressively project its unwavering Atlanticist loyalty, sending a clear signal to Washington that Rome remains firmly tough on Moscow.

Moving from geopolitical espionage to a slow-moving domestic timebomb, the National Social Security Institute, or INPS, has released its annual report, and the economic picture is grim. The data reveals that Italians are now retiring an average of seven years and three months later than they did in 1995. While the sheer number of working pensioners is increasing, wages remain incredibly fragile, and a stark gender divide persists. Women make up the majority of pensioners, yet their monthly checks are on average 30 percent lower than men's due to decades of systemic wage gaps and interrupted careers. It is a stark reminder that beneath the daily political noise, the mathematical reality of Italy's aging demographic continues to heavily strain the state's welfare machine.

Staying in the realm of domestic policy, a fierce legislative battle is brewing in Parliament over who gets to be Italian. The Chamber of Deputies has approved a fast-track procedure for a hardline bill proposed by the Lega that would severely tighten citizenship requirements and expedite the repatriation of convicted immigrants. The political dynamic here is fascinating. Il Manifesto and the progressive left describe this as the "Vannacci effect," arguing that Matteo Salvini's Lega is desperately sprinting to the extreme right to appease the followers of ultra-conservative General Roberto Vannacci. Conversely, the right-wing press, including Il Giornale, applauds the maneuver, framing the crackdown as a necessary, common-sense measure to guarantee national security and deport foreign criminals.
