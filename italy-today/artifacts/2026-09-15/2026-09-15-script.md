The Senate has just given the green light to a sweeping new electoral law, passing the bill with 113 votes. The legislation, which now heads to the lower house for final approval, introduces a proportional system with preference voting, but its most critical feature is a massive majority premium. Any coalition that reaches 42 percent of the national vote will automatically be handed a 70-seat bonus in the Chamber of Deputies and 35 extra seats in the Senate. 

The framing of this vote could not be more polarized. The progressive establishment, heavily amplified by La Repubblica and La Stampa, is branding this a "scam law." Opposition senators literally held up yellow and red protest signs in the chamber, arguing that the right-wing coalition is manipulating the math to ensure a permanent grip on power, even if they lack an absolute majority of the popular vote. Conversely, the sovereigntist press, including La Verità and Libero, is defending the reform as a historic victory for governability. They argue that Italy can no longer afford its decades-long tradition of fragile, revolving-door governments, especially in an era where global stability is fractured. The takeaway is clear: the government is moving aggressively to institutionalize its power, betting that Italians care more about stability than pure proportional representation.

Moving from the halls of the Senate to the vaults of Italy's largest bank, a massive institutional security breach has just been exposed. Prosecutors in Bari have closed their investigation into a former Intesa Sanpaolo employee who spent years illegally accessing the sensitive financial data of over three thousand high-profile clients. We are not just talking about celebrities; this rogue banker spied on the personal accounts of President Sergio Mattarella, Premier Giorgia Meloni, and former European Central Bank Chief Mario Draghi. While Il Fatto Quotidiano questions the systemic cyber vulnerabilities of the private banking sector, the conservative press is treating this as a severe threat to national security, demanding to know if this was mere curiosity or coordinated political espionage.

Turning to the real economy, and following up on yesterday's look at the 2027 budget maneuver, the government is trying to find a lifeline for Italy's struggling small businesses. Il Sole 24 Ore is reporting that the Finance Ministry is eyeing a flat tax, or "cedolare secca," on commercial rent. With over 1.5 million small shops at risk of being choked out by inflation and online retail, this tax cut is designed to incentivize landlords to lower rents for brick-and-mortar stores. However, just like yesterday's proposed tax cuts for freelancers, finding the budget cover for this move while keeping Brussels happy will be a monumental diplomatic task.
