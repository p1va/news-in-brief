Following up on our lead story from yesterday, the fundamental rewiring of the Italian democratic machine is barreling forward. The government successfully won its third consecutive confidence vote in the Chamber of Deputies, securing 228 votes to lock down Article 3 of the highly contested "Melonellum" electoral law. The new legislation is poised to reintroduce a modified preference voting system, but crucially, it keeps the top candidates on the party lists strictly blocked, guaranteeing safe seats for party loyalists. 

The media framing of this final sprint is fiercely divided. *Il Fatto Quotidiano* points out that the ruling coalition is holding its breath for tomorrow's final, secret ballot, noting that the anonymity of the vote is the only remaining ambush point for internal dissenters. *La Repubblica* continues to decry the rushed timeline as an authoritarian steamroller. Meanwhile, *Il Sole 24 Ore* bypasses the political outrage entirely, offering a dry, technical breakdown of how the hybrid preference system will actually function at the ballot box. The takeaway is clear: barring a catastrophic mutiny in tomorrow's secret vote, the right-wing coalition is days away from cementing the mathematical rules of engagement for the next general election.

Moving from the halls of parliament to the nation's hospitals, Italy is facing a massive contradiction in public health. Today, medical unions unanimously signed a new contract granting 138,000 public healthcare directors and doctors monthly raises of up to 560 euros, along with new protections against workplace violence. Government-aligned voices celebrate this as a kept promise to frontline workers. However, the independent Gimbe Foundation simultaneously released a devastating report showing that between inflation and systemic cuts, the national health service has effectively lost 18.8 billion euros, forcing millions of Italians to abandon necessary medical care. *Il Manifesto* weaponizes this data, accusing the Meloni administration's austerity measures of quietly dismantling Italy's universal healthcare model even as it hands out targeted pay bumps to stave off strikes.

Turning to the justice system, President Sergio Mattarella has issued three presidential pardons, one of which is reigniting a deeply polarized debate on self-defense. Mattarella granted a partial pardon to Guido Gianni, a jeweler sentenced to over twelve years in prison for killing two robbers and wounding a third during a 2008 heist. Because Gianni has already served over five years, the pardon reduces his remaining sentence just enough to qualify for social probation. *Libero* and the conservative press are treating this as a massive cultural victory, vindicating the right to armed defense of private property. Progressive outlets, however, are carefully analyzing the institutional mechanics, noting that Mattarella acted strictly on the recommendation of Justice Minister Carlo Nordio, signaling a subtle but highly consequential shift in the state's legal posture toward lethal self-defense in the workplace.

But before we go, researchers are flocking to the small Sardinian town of Bolotana. According to *Il Sole 24 Ore*, the island's famous "Blue Zone"—areas globally renowned for populations that live exceptionally long and healthy lives—is officially expanding. Demographers have identified a brand new cluster of centenarian women in the mountainous town of just over two thousand residents. It is a welcome reminder that while the rest of the world stresses over geopolitical crises, artificial intelligence, and economic deficits, the secret to a long life might just be found in the slow pace and tight-knit communities of the Sardinian hills.
