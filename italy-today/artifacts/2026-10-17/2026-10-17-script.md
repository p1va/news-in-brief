Down south, the Ex Ilva steel plant in Taranto has announced a massive expansion of its furlough scheme, effectively freezing its workforce. In Rome, the CGIL trade union has officially called a general strike for November 20th. Within the government, tensions over the upcoming budget are boiling over as Deputy Prime Minister Matteo Salvini doubles down on demanding a 5-billion-euro levy from the banks, prompting pushback from Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani. Abroad, US President Donald Trump has issued an executive order halting key sanctions and tariffs on Russia. And finally, simmering anger over the cost of living has exploded into urban warfare across Italy and France, which brings us to our lead story.

What began as a weekend of decentralized, grassroots protests against inflation and the soaring cost of living quickly degenerated into violent clashes across Northern Italy today. In Milan, Turin, and Bologna, anti-establishment collectives hijacked the marches, transforming city centers into battlegrounds. In Milan's Piazza Duomo, police deployed water cannons and tear gas after being pelted with paper bombs, cobblestones, and even restaurant tables. Dozens of arrests have been made, mirroring similar scenes of chaos and arson that paralyzed Paris, Lyon, and Marseille today. Meanwhile, in Rome, the landscape was vastly different: tens of thousands gathered peacefully for a massive rally organized by the CGIL union, where leader Maurizio Landini, joined by opposition leaders Elly Schlein and Giuseppe Conte, formally announced a general strike for next month. 

The media spin perfectly encapsulates a fractured country. The right-wing press, led by *La Verità* and *Libero*, is dedicating its front pages entirely to the urban guerrilla warfare. They frame the protesters as violent hooligans holding cities hostage and prominently feature Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni’s sharp attack on the center-left, where she demanded that the opposition unequivocally condemn the riots, stating that "every reticence becomes a justification." Conversely, *La Repubblica* and *Il Fatto Quotidiano* are minimizing the clashes to focus heavily on the peaceful CGIL rally in Rome. They frame the broader street anger as a legitimate, inevitable boiling over of social despair caused by the government's failure to tame inflation, protect wages, and tax corporate profits. 

The takeaway here is that the political temperature in Italy is reaching a boiling point ahead of the autumn budget season. The government is attempting to corner the opposition by associating their economic platforms with street violence, while the center-left is finally trying to consolidate a unified front with the unions. But with similar anti-inflation riots spreading across Europe, the underlying economic anxiety is proving impossible for anyone to control.

Turning from the streets to the factories, the industrial crisis in the south has just deepened. Acciaierie d’Italia, the company managing the troubled Ex Ilva steelworks, has officially requested a staggering expansion of the "cassa integrazione"—the state-funded furlough scheme. Out of roughly 9,600 employees across Taranto, Genoa, and Novi, over 8,600 will be suspended until at least February 2027. *Il Sole 24 Ore* notes that the shutdown of the hot area in the Taranto plant made this move inevitable. While unions are calling the decision an unacceptable provocation, it exposes a grim reality: despite endless government interventions, Italy is systematically failing to keep its heavy manufacturing base alive in the face of insurmountable operational costs.

Meanwhile, on the international stage, a major geopolitical shift from Washington is sending shockwaves through European defense circles. US President Donald Trump has signed an executive order suspending critical export sanctions and tariffs against Russia, effectively dismantling the recently approved Lindsey Graham Act. While the Kremlin is openly celebrating this as a "historic moment," *La Stampa* points out that the move removes the threat of steep tariffs on nations like China and India that purchase Russian oil, essentially unblocking Moscow's war chest. The pivot drew immediate, public ire from Rome. Italian Defense Minister Guido Crosetto took the rare step of openly criticizing the Trump administration today, warning that American attempts to limit satellite and intelligence access to Kyiv is a grave error that will only convince Vladimir Putin to continue his advance. 

But before we go, a piece of good news for Italy's cultural heritage. After sixteen long years of closures, occupations, and bureaucratic limbo, Rome's historic Teatro Valle has finally reopened its doors. Dating back to the 18th century, and famous for hosting the premiere of Pirandello's "Six Characters in Search of an Author" in 1921, the theater has been fully restored and inaugurated today by the mayor. *Il Manifesto* and the local press are celebrating the rebirth of this architectural gem, noting that amidst the noise of the modern capital, Rome has finally recovered one of its most vital and historic artistic voices.
