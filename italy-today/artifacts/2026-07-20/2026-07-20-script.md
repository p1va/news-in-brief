Moving to our lead story, the tragic death of a 43-year-old man during a police intervention in Bologna has escalated from a local controversy into a major national test of the government's justice reforms. As thousands marched through Bologna demanding truth for Abderrahim Fakir, the local prosecutor made a monumental legal decision: rather than placing the six involved officers and paramedics under formal investigation, they applied the brand-new "scudo penale," or penal shield.

This mechanism, technically known as "Model 45 bis," was just introduced by the Meloni government's latest Security Decree. As Il Messaggero and Libero explain, it creates a separate legal registry that prevents law enforcement from being automatically labeled as suspects when a death occurs in the line of duty, provided there is an apparent cause of justification. The right-wing press defends this as a necessary safeguard to allow police to do their jobs without the constant fear of career-ruining indictments.

The opposition, however, is furious. La Stampa and Il Fatto Quotidiano are framing the use of the shield as a state-sanctioned cover-up, pointing to the viral video of Fakir pinned face-down and handcuffed crying for help. The takeaway here is profound: this is the very first high-profile stress test of the new Security Decree, and it is exposing a deep ideological fracture over who the state prioritizes protecting in moments of crisis—its citizens or its uniforms.
