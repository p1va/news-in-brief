The surging cost of energy is exactly what triggered an emergency summit at Palazzo Chigi today. Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni and Economy Minister Giancarlo Giorgetti are scrambling to address the "caro-benzina"—the sudden spike in fuel prices hitting millions of Italians right as the summer holiday exodus begins. The government is urgently exploring the use of "accise mobili," a sliding-scale mechanism that automatically cuts state excise duties on gas when oil prices jump, essentially using the extra VAT revenue to subsidize the cost at the pump. 

The political spin around this intervention is fiercely divided. *La Repubblica* and the progressive press frame the summit as a frantic damage-control operation, eager to remind readers that the right-wing coalition once campaigned on entirely abolishing fuel taxes—a populist promise they quickly abandoned once in power. Conversely, *Il Giornale* and the government's defenders argue that the executive is successfully shielding families from external, uncontrollable shocks, specifically pointing to the escalating conflict in the Middle East as the true culprit behind the rising crude prices. 

The ultimate takeaway here is that Italy's fragile economic recovery is highly exposed. Between Confindustria begging for liquidity loans to help factories pay energy bills, the European Central Bank refusing to lower interest rates, and Trump’s new trade tariffs threatening to squeeze Italian exports, Meloni's economic maneuvering room ahead of the autumn budget is shrinking fast.

Moving from the economy to the limits of ideological legislation, the Meloni government’s highly publicized crackdown on "cannabis light" is quietly hitting a brick wall in the Italian courts. As *Il Post* details today, the recent decree criminalizing the production and sale of CBD products is failing the stress test of the judicial system. While the sovereigntist right championed the ban as a victory for public morals and a hardline stance against drugs, local tribunals are increasingly dismissing the charges against shop owners. The judges point out that the Italian ban outright conflicts with European Union free-market directives on agricultural products. It highlights a recurring pattern for this government: passing highly visible, headline-grabbing identity laws that ultimately get dismantled by local magistrates applying overriding European law.

Speaking of headline-grabbing laws, the political warfare over yesterday's juvenile justice decree has escalated into a full-blown messaging war. Following up on yesterday's report about the government's move to make 14-year-olds legally culpable by default, the center-left Democratic Party has launched a shock ad campaign. Their new posters feature a photo of the Prime Minister with the stark slogan, "With Meloni, your children can go to jail." Meloni fired back immediately today, stating that her government is simply making Italy safer for those who actually respect the rules. The exchange perfectly encapsulates the current political climate: complex, systemic issues involving marginalized youth and organized crime are being reduced to binary, highly weaponized slogans by both the government and the opposition.

A groundbreaking new bill has just landed in the Italian Senate, drafted in collaboration with the Italian Society of Pediatrics. The proposal seeks to legally ban the use of smartphones, tablets, and all digital devices for children under three years old. If passed, it would shift the responsibility of "digital detox" from exhausted parents to a national health and educational mandate. So, if you’re heading to the beach this weekend and hoping to use a screen to keep a toddler quiet under the umbrella, enjoy it while it lasts—the Italian state might soon step in to take the tablet away.
