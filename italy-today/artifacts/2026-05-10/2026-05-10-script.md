The political framing of this crisis is deeply polarized. The progressive opposition press, led by La Repubblica and La Stampa, portrays this as a disastrous failure of Meloni’s foreign policy. On the other side, conservative papers like Il Giornale are attempting to extinguish the fire, heavily platforming Defense Minister Guido Crosetto's reassurances that there will be no "tearing up" of historical agreements. They note that Palazzo Chigi has remained officially silent, framing the lack of a direct response from Meloni as a strategy of prudent diplomacy rather than weakness.

The undeniable takeaway is that Italy's traditional geopolitical shield is cracking. With the US imposing tariffs and now openly questioning its military umbrella over the Mediterranean, Rome is being forced to realize that the old rules of the Atlantic alliance no longer apply, leaving the government entirely exposed to Washington's shifting moods.

Moving from international pressures to domestic economic relief, the government has launched a financial lifeline for struggling businesses. Starting May 25th, small and medium-sized enterprises that have suffered significant energy cost increases or revenue drops will be able to apply for emergency support. It is a vital injection of liquidity, but economists warn it remains a temporary patch on a much larger structural deficit.

While Il Fatto Quotidiano and Fanpage highlight this as a chaotic internal war over ideological control and historical narratives, the establishment center-right is scrambling to minimize the fallout, treating it as a mere administrative reshuffle. However, the optics of infighting over the memory of Regeni is a political headache the Prime Minister did not need.
