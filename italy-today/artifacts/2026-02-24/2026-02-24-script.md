Welcome to another episode of "Italy Today", today is Tuesday 24 February 2026 and this episode was curated by Gemini 3 Pro.

We begin with the Blitz.

It is a grim anniversary in Eastern Europe. Today marks exactly four years since the Russian invasion of Ukraine. While *Corriere della Sera* reports that President Zelensky is declaring that "Putin has failed," the mood in the Italian Senate is far more somber. The government is forcing a confidence vote to renew military aid, pushing back against what *Il Fatto Quotidiano* describes as widespread "war fatigue" and a fracturing opposition. Meanwhile, the drug war in Mexico has confirmed its latest casualty; authorities have positively identified the body of "El Mencho," the leader of the Jalisco cartel, with *La Stampa* noting that while the King is dead, the violence has only metastasized across the region.

Closer to home, a new technological barrier has been erected for Italian drivers. As of this morning, the "Alcolock" law is in force. *Il Sole 24 Ore* details that repeat offenders for drunk driving must now install a device that prevents the engine from starting if alcohol is detected on their breath—a safety measure that comes with a stinging price tag of 2,000 euros, payable entirely by the driver. And finally, in the US, the "Cyclone Bomb" snowstorm has paralyzed the Northeast, with *Ansa* reporting over 60 million people under alert and air traffic with Europe still in chaotic disarray.

The lead story today is the catastrophic collapse of the "self-defense" narrative in Northern Italy, as two major judicial cases explode simultaneously, putting the populist Right on the defensive.

We start in Milan, where the case of the police officer Carmelo Cinturrino has moved from investigation to confession. Following yesterday's report on his arrest for killing a drug dealer in the Rogoredo grove, *Fanpage* and *La Repubblica* report that the officer has admitted to the investigating judge that he "lost his head" and, crucially, staged the crime scene. Cinturrino confessed to planting a playful gun next to the victim's body to simulate self-defense, confirming the prosecution's theory of a frame-up. The sheer weight of this admission has forced a dramatic pivot in the government-aligned press.

Usually, the instinct of *Libero* and *Il Giornale* is to circle the wagons around law enforcement. However, faced with a confession of evidence tampering, the tone has shifted from defense to distance. The most significant reaction comes from Senate President Ignazio La Russa, a historic figure of the Right. As reported by *Adnkronos*, La Russa stated that if the facts are confirmed, he demands "double severity" because the perpetrator wore a uniform. This is a massive departure from the "always with the police" rhetoric of the past few years, signaling that the establishment is cutting Cinturrino loose to save face.

But the judicial blow to the "Wild West" mentality didn't end in Milan. In a separate but thematically identical story, *La Stampa* leads with the sentencing of Massimo Adriatici, the former Lega security assessor in Voghera. Five years after shooting a homeless man during an altercation, Adriatici has been convicted of voluntary homicide and sentenced to 12 years in prison. The defense had argued for legitimate defense, a cornerstone of Matteo Salvini’s political platform, but the court rejected it outright. *Il Fatto Quotidiano* juxtaposes today's sentence with Salvini's 2021 declaration that the shooting was "clear self-defense," highlighting a widening chasm between populist political promises and the hard reality of the Italian penal code.

Moving from the courts to the corridors of academia, where a new scandal is fueling the "waste of public money" fire.

The European Public Prosecutor’s Office (EPPO) has requested the arrest of sixteen university professors and researchers across Sicily and Campania, accusing them of treating EU research funds as a personal ATM. According to *Il Sole 24 Ore*, the investigation reveals a "Magna Magna" scheme where funds earmarked for digital innovation and research were siphoned off to purchase iPhones, smart TVs, and computers for personal use.

The spin here is predictable but damaging. *Libero* frames this as a confirmation of the inherent corruption in the southern academic complex, while *Repubblica* focuses on the institutional embarrassment. With the Trump administration already looking for excuses to apply tariffs and Brussels tightening its belt, *Corriere della Sera* notes that this is exactly the kind of story Italy does not need: highly educated civil servants caught looting the very European funds that are supposed to modernize the nation's economy.

Turning to the shifting sands of the European Right.

General Roberto Vannacci, the polarizing figure who rode a wave of anti-woke sentiment into the European Parliament, has made a move that is causing headaches for Premier Meloni. *La Stampa* reports that Vannacci has officially joined the "Europe of Sovereign Nations" group, sitting alongside the German AfD. This places him to the right of Meloni’s Conservatives and even Le Pen’s Patriots.

*Il Giornale* treats this with caution, viewing it as a strategic error that isolates the General in a "cordon sanitaire" within Brussels. By aligning with the AfD—who are currently political pariahs in Chancellor Merz’s Germany—Vannacci is complicating Meloni’s diplomatic tightrope walk. The takeaway is clear: while Meloni tries to present herself as a moderate institutionalist to survive the Trump-Merz axis, her coalition partners are drifting toward the fringes, creating a liability she may soon have to address.

But before we go, the nation prepares to suspend its disbelief and its sleep schedule.

Tonight marks the opening of the 76th Sanremo Festival. *Corriere della Sera* reports that Carlo Conti is back at the helm, promising a "Christian and democratic" festival—a tongue-in-cheek reassurance after years of controversy. The streets of the Ligurian city are packed, Tiziano Ferro is the super-guest, and despite the wars, the tariffs, and the political scandals, for the next five nights, the only voting Italians will care about involves televoting for their favorite song. As *La Repubblica* notes, it is the one week of the year where the country manages to agree on absolutely nothing, together.
