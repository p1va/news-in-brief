In Washington, the constitutional clash has escalated into a full-blown trade war. In Milan, the Winter Olympics have delivered a moment of pure cinematic emotion. On the snow of Livigno, Italy achieved a historic "one-two" in ski cross, with Simone Deromedis taking gold and Federico Tomasoni grabbing silver. *La Repubblica* notes that the victory was overshadowed by a heartbreaking tribute: Tomasoni raced with a sun painted on his helmet in memory of his girlfriend, skier Matilde Lorenzi, who died in a training accident in 2024, dedicating the medal to her in a tearful post-race interview.

However, the mood at Rai is considerably darker. The state broadcaster is apologizing profusely after a hot mic caught a commentator during the bobsleigh event saying, "Let's avoid the Israeli crew." *Libero* and *La Stampa* report that while Rai Sport executives called the comment "unacceptable" and promised an internal investigation, the Jewish Community of Milan has branded the incident "shameful," marking yet another controversy for the public network's Olympic coverage.

Shocking scenes on the short track ice, where the Polish skater Kamila Sellier narrowly escaped tragedy. During the 1500 meters quarterfinals, a collision sent a skate blade slicing across her face. *Corriere della Sera* confirms she was rushed to surgery with a severe cut to the cheek, narrowly missing her eye, sparking immediate debates about the mandatory use of cut-resistant visors.

And finally, a grim sense of déjà vu in Bologna. The 29-year-old man who struck and killed Ettore Pausini—uncle of pop star Laura Pausini—last November has been arrested again. In a twist that defies belief, *Il Resto del Carlino* reports he was caught stealing from a florist on the exact same street where the fatal accident occurred, reigniting local anger over recidivism and judicial leniency.

The lead story today is the tragic conclusion to a medical scandal that has held Naples in a grip of sorrow and outrage for two months.

This morning at 9:20 AM, little Domenico died. The two-year-old boy, who had been kept alive by machinery at the Monaldi Hospital since December 23rd, finally succumbed to organ failure. His death transforms a case of malpractice into a homicide investigation. Domenico had received a heart transplant just before Christmas, but the donor organ had been transported incorrectly—packed in dry ice, which froze and effectively "burned" the tissue, rendering it useless. As *AGI* reports, the shift in the legal landscape was immediate: the Naples prosecutor has upgraded the file from "negligent lesions" to "manslaughter" and ordered the seizure of six mobile phones belonging to the medical staff involved in the transport chain.

The reaction has been a mixture of immense public grief and defensive posturing. While Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni tweeted that "all of Italy embraces the family," promising that authorities will shed full light on the matter, the institutional machinery is already grinding towards a complex legal battle. *La Repubblica* highlights the heart-wrenching statement from Domenico's mother, Patrizia, who vowed that her son "will not be forgotten," but the paper also points to the systemic failure: how could a leading European transplant center make such a rudimentary error with temperature control?

The synthesis of this story lies in the technological gap exposed by the tragedy. While the public focus is on human error, *Il Sole 24 Ore* and *AGI* point to a structural deficit. The "dry ice" method is 40 years old. The spotlight is now turning to why the Italian healthcare system has been slow to adopt modern "warm perfusion" technologies—machines that keep the heart beating during transport and could have prevented this thermal shock. Domenico’s death is likely to accelerate the adoption of these costly but vital tools, a shift that comes too late for one family in Nola.

Turning to a story developing in the gray zone between law enforcement and organized crime.

The investigation into the shooting at the "Rogoredo grove"—stopped initially as a case of self-defense—is rapidly disintegrating into a tale of corruption. *La Stampa* and *Fanpage* are reporting explosive allegations against Carmelo Cinturrino, the police officer who shot and killed a drug dealer named Mansouri last month. Witnesses and associates of the victim are now telling prosecutors that the officer was not merely patrolling the drug-infested park, but was actively extorting the dealers, allegedly demanding 200 euros and 5 grams of cocaine a day as a "pizzo" or protection fee. If proven, this fundamentally changes the narrative from a "tragic operational error" to a premeditated execution to cover up extortion, a theory bolstered by the fact that Cinturrino's own colleagues have turned against him, accusing him of staging the crime scene.

Meanwhile, a chilling report from across the Atlantic is forcing a reckoning on the ethics of Artificial Intelligence.

*Corriere della Sera*, picking up a *Wall Street Journal* exclusive, reveals that OpenAI—the creator of ChatGPT—knew about a mass shooter's intentions months before she pulled the trigger. Jesse Van Rootselaar, who killed eight people in Canada earlier this month, had reportedly used the AI chatbot to fantasize about and plan the massacre in detail eight months prior. The revelation is that while OpenAI's safety systems "flagged" the user, the company did not alert law enforcement, judging that there was no "imminent" threat. This story is set to become a cornerstone in the European debate on AI regulation, challenging the "black box" immunity of tech giants when their algorithms become confidants to murderers.

But before we go, let’s leave the darker side of humanity for a moment of ecological redemption.

150 years after they were eaten into extinction by hungry sailors and whalers, giant tortoises are walking once again on the island of Floreana in the Galápagos. *Il Post* reports that not the original species, but a genetically similar cousin bred in captivity, has been reintroduced to the wild. 150 plodding giants were released this week, tasked with acting as "ecosystem engineers" to trample the brush and restore the island's natural balance. It is a slow, steady, and wrinkled reminder that sometimes, with enough patience and science, we can actually fix what we broke.
