Our main story today takes us into the rapidly evolving, and increasingly militarized, future of Italian defense. In a highly unusual geopolitical move, Italian Defense Minister Guido Crosetto has officially hired Mykhailo Fedorov—the 35-year-old former Ukrainian Defense Minister recently ousted by Volodymyr Zelensky—as his new special advisor for defense innovation. Fedorov, widely known as Kyiv’s "drone wizard," has been tasked with helping Italy adapt to modern, high-tech warfare, specifically focusing on unmanned systems and anti-aircraft infrastructure.

The framing of this appointment heavily depends on which paper you read. La Stampa and Corriere della Sera treat the move as a pragmatic necessity, quoting Fedorov's promise to share Ukraine’s "unique and concrete experience" in high-tech warfare to help Rome modernize. They view this as a vital step for Italy to build a more robust European defense model, especially in the wake of the diplomatic shockwaves caused by the Trump administration's threats to NATO earlier this year. 

However, the opposition press sees a more troubling subtext. Il Fatto Quotidiano emphasizes Fedorov's controversial removal by Zelensky, questioning the vetting of the appointment. Meanwhile, Il Manifesto paints the move as a disturbing ideological shift, warning that the Meloni government is essentially using Ukraine’s perpetual state of conflict as the new baseline model for Italian defense. Ultimately, the takeaway is clear: Italy is no longer just funding traditional military assets, but actively importing wartime leadership to overhaul its national security apparatus.

Moving from military strategy to a massive domestic political storm, the battle over Italy's state broadcaster has reached a boiling point. The RAI network has officially removed veteran investigative journalist Sigfrido Ranucci from the helm of the flagship program Report. In what is being widely described as a provocation, management offered Ranucci a reassignment as a correspondent in Cairo—an offer he flatly rejected, citing the fate of Giulio Regeni. The program will now be hosted by two younger, internal journalists, Giulia Presutti and Daniele Piervincenzi. 

La Repubblica and Il Fatto Quotidiano are pulling no punches, framing the ouster as a calculated political purge by the Meloni government to silence its most effective media critic. The entire reporting staff of Report has threatened a mass resignation, calling the new appointments an "unacceptable" political mandate. Conversely, the center-right Il Giornale and government allies defend the shakeup as a long-overdue return to meritocracy, arguing that Ranucci had turned the public broadcast into a partisan weapon. The clash highlights a deep, ongoing institutional war over the independence of Italian state media.

Turning to the economy, a landmark agreement has just reshaped the gig economy in Italy. Il Sole 24 Ore reports that food delivery giant Deliveroo has struck a deal with Milan prosecutors to raise the minimum hourly wage for its riders from 10 to 14 euros. This comes as the company attempts to exit a judicial administration order imposed earlier this year over illegal labor exploitation. The progressive press, including Il Manifesto, cautiously celebrates the wage hike as a victory against the "caporalato" system, noting that Deliveroo is desperately trying to avoid the fate of its rival, Glovo, whose own restructuring plan was rejected by judges just two days ago. It’s a structural victory for labor rights that sets a new legal precedent for algorithmic gig work across the country.
