Our lead story tonight takes us inside the halls of the Economy Ministry, where the battle lines for Italy's next financial maneuver are being drawn. Economy Minister Giancarlo Giorgetti took the stage today to outline the government's priorities for the 2027 Budget Law. He proposed a specialized "flat tax" specifically targeting salary increases for young workers, aiming to incentivize companies to raise wages. Furthermore, he opened the door to allowing early retirement at age 64, but added a heavy caveat: it can only happen if it guarantees the equilibrium of public finances. 

The synthesis of this financial roadmap changes dramatically depending on where you look. Il Sole 24 Ore and Il Giornale frame Giorgetti's speech as a pragmatic, pro-business agenda, praising the government's attempt to cut taxes while navigating the rigid new fiscal rules imposed by the European Union. However, Il Fatto Quotidiano and La Repubblica are focusing on the explosive internal fractures these promises reveal. They highlight a bitter, ongoing clash within the coalition: the League is pushing hard for a new tax on the extra-profits of banks to fund these measures, a move that Forza Italia and the moderates categorically refuse to support. 

The takeaway here is that Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni is facing a severe mathematical reality check. The government wants to project unity and deliver on populist promises like tax cuts and early retirement, but with Brussels watching closely, finding the actual cash to fund these pledges without triggering an internal coalition war will be the defining political test of the autumn.

Turning to the state of our schools, the latest OECD PISA 2025 report was released today, and it paints a complex picture of the Italian education system. Globally, the report shows a historic and alarming drop in student performance across OECD nations, largely attributed to the post-pandemic landscape, excessive screen time, and the integration of AI. However, Italian 15-year-olds bucked the trend, scoring significantly above the international average in reading, math, and science. Minister of Education Giuseppe Valditara immediately celebrated the results, telling conservative outlets like La Verità that the Italian school system is incredibly solid and rapidly climbing global rankings. But ANSA and progressive outlets are reading the fine print, noting that while the academic scores are up, the report exposes a grim social reality: Italian students report some of the highest levels of pessimism and a severe lack of belonging in their classrooms.
