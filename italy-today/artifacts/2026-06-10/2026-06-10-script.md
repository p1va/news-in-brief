Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni took the stage at the Confcommercio assembly today to deliver a staunchly pro-business manifesto. The fact is straightforward: Meloni promised further tax cuts for the middle class, vehemently rejected any notion of a wealth tax, and proudly announced the forced closure of twenty-four thousand "open-and-close" shell companies, primarily run by foreign nationals to evade taxes. The spin across the right-wing press is triumphant. Il Giornale and Libero are cheering her declaration that Italy is not a banana republic, framing her speech as a robust defense of wealth creators against the left's perpetual desire to tax the rich.

However, the progressive opposition is pointing out a glaring paradox in the government's fiscal strategy. As La Repubblica and Fanpage are highlighting today, while Meloni preaches a light-touch state, her administration is simultaneously rolling out a massive expansion of the tax authority's powers. Under the new Simplification Decree, the traditional F24 tax form is effectively being retired. Instead, the Revenue Agency will now have the power to withdraw taxes directly from citizens' bank accounts via an automated direct debit system. While the government frames this as a bureaucratic convenience akin to paying a utility bill, critics see it as an unprecedented intrusion of the state into private finances. The takeaway here is a classic political tightrope: the right-wing coalition must project an anti-tax, pro-freedom image to its base, even as the harsh realities of Italy's public debt force them to implement incredibly aggressive, automated tax collection systems.

Moving from the economy to the structural crisis in Italian healthcare, a much-anticipated decree to reform Italy's family doctors has spectacularly imploded before even reaching the cabinet. The Ministry of Health had proposed moving independent general practitioners into state-run "Community Houses" as public employees. This sparked open rebellion. Il Fatto Quotidiano reports that Lombardy's health chief, Guido Bertolaso, furiously abandoned the negotiating table, calling the situation demeaning. The collapse of this reform leaves Italy's primary care system exactly where it started: severely understaffed, disjointed from the hospital network, and paralyzed by a turf war between the central government, regional authorities, and doctors' unions.

Meanwhile, on the law enforcement front, the cabinet has officially approved a new legislative decree integrating Artificial Intelligence into police work. According to wire service ANSA, the decree adapts Italian law to the European AI Act. Interior Minister Matteo Piantedosi spent his press conference attempting to soothe privacy advocates, explicitly stating that the AI will act as a support tool, not an automated policeman, and promising that human oversight will always remain in place. However, La Stampa and La Repubblica are already raising the alarm over exactly who will manage these databases and the potential use of biometric data, setting the stage for a major civil liberties battle in the coming months over how predictive policing will be deployed on Italian streets.
