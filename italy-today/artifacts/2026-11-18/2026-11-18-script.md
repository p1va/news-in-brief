Turning to our main story, the government's 2027 budget law is officially under siege. As the deadline for parliamentary amendments closed, over 6,600 proposed changes flooded the budget committee. But the true lightning rod is a push by the ruling majority—specifically Fratelli d'Italia and Forza Italia—to include a sweeping *condono edilizio*, or building amnesty. The proposal would allow homeowners to pay a fee to legalize minor zoning and construction violations, effectively wiping the slate clean for millions of irregular modifications. 

The political framing around this move highlights a classic Italian divide. On the right, the government defends the measure as a pragmatic necessity. As noted in *La Stampa*, defenders of the amnesty argue it will cut through paralyzing local bureaucracy, free up the real estate market, and bring much-needed cash into state coffers. However, the opposition and progressive outlets like *La Repubblica* and *Il Fatto Quotidiano* are absolutely torching the proposal. They frame it as the government winking at tax evaders and illegal builders, warning that a budget law should not become a vehicle for legalizing illegality just to scrape the barrel for funds. 

The structural takeaway here is that Italy's chronic struggle between rigid state regulations and widespread informal rule-breaking remains unresolved. Even Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni is reportedly concerned that branding this budget as the "amnesty budget" could politically backfire, especially as the European Commission projects Italy's public debt to hit a record 137.9% of GDP this coming year. 

Moving from parliamentary battles to the digital lives of Italian families, the government is about to fundamentally alter how young people access the internet. Prime Minister Meloni is fast-tracking a major amendment to the upcoming Security Decree that will completely ban social media access for children under 13. For teenagers between 13 and 16, platforms will only be accessible through an account directly linked to a parent's digital ID. While *Il Manifesto* criticizes the move as an authoritarian overreach that ignores the complexities of cyberbullying and education, institutional consensus is building rapidly. The government is pushing to pass the law by the end of November, effectively front-running the European Union's broader digital guidelines.

In another major shift for consumers, the era of frictionless online debt is coming to a screeching halt. As reported by *Il Sole 24 Ore*, starting this Friday, strict new Bank of Italy regulations will officially classify "Buy Now, Pay Later" services like Klarna and Scalapay as standard consumer credit. Previously operating in a regulatory gray area, these wildly popular apps will now be legally required to perform rigorous credit checks before approving a transaction. With the Bank of Italy noting that the use of these apps has skyrocketed from 4% to 30% of households in just three years—often trapping lower-income buyers in a spiral of hidden debt—the state is stepping in to ensure that dividing a payment in three is no longer just a simple click, but a regulated financial contract.

But before we go, the Italian pastry world is already gearing up for Christmas 2026, and tradition is getting a structural upgrade. At the annual "Sweets of the Holidays" showcase in Naples, master pastry chefs unveiled recipes designed to adapt heavy historical desserts to modern palates. The standout? An ancient monastic technique for making the spiced *mostacciolo* cookie, where the dough is left to mature for a full 40 days before baking, allowing natural enzymes to create a softer, richer texture. It is a reminder that in Italy, true culinary innovation often just means remembering exactly how things were done centuries ago.
