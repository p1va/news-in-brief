We begin with the rapid-fire updates.

In Washington, President Donald Trump has delivered the longest State of the Union address in history, declaring a new "Golden Age" for America and defending his aggressive tariff policies as necessary despite Supreme Court pushback, though *La Repubblica* notes the deep divide in the chamber. Closer to home, yet another stain has appeared on the Italian healthcare system; in Naples, authorities have arrested four people after revealing that the Clan Contini camorra syndicate was effectively running the San Giovanni Bosco hospital, controlling everything from the ambulance bay to the vending machines. Meanwhile, the gig economy faces a judicial reckoning in Milan, where Deliveroo has been placed under judicial administration for "caporalato," or illegal labor intermediation, with prosecutors alleging riders were paid ninety percent below the poverty line. In Parliament, the government has shot down the opposition's proposal for equal parental leave, with *Il Giornale* citing the State Accounting Office’s verdict that the measure is financially "unsustainable." And finally, the hangover from the Ariston Theatre is real: the debut of the 76th Sanremo Festival saw a sharp ratings drop, losing three million viewers compared to last year's premiere, confirming that the post-Amadeus era is off to a rocky start.

Following up on yesterday's report regarding the police shooting in Rogoredo, the situation has escalated from a judicial news item to a full-blown institutional crisis.

Yesterday, we reported that police officer Carmelo Cinturrino confessed to staging a crime scene after shooting a drug dealer in Milan. Today, the institutions of the State remain in shock, but the reaction from the top has been swift and ruthless. In a rare and explosive interview with *Corriere della Sera*, the Chief of Police, Vittorio Pisani, did not mince words. Breaking the traditional "blue wall of silence," Pisani declared that the officer must be "destituted immediately," bypassing the usual wait for a final court verdict. Pisani effectively argued that mistaking a reaction for self-defense is one thing, but planting a gun to frame a corpse is a betrayal of the uniform that requires immediate excision.

This hardline stance from the police leadership is forcing the political Right to recalibrate its "law and order" messaging in real-time. While *La Repubblica* and *Fanpage* focus on the investigating judge's decision to keep Cinturrino in prison—citing a risk that he might "inhabit the crime scene" or intimidate witnesses—the government-aligned press is navigating a difficult pivot. *Libero* and *Il Giornale*, usually the first to defend the actions of uniformed officers, are aligning with Senate President Ignazio La Russa’s call for "double severity." The takeaway here is significant: the Meloni government, currently pushing for greater legal protections for police, is terrified that this "bad apple" could rot the entire barrel of their security reforms. By cutting Cinturrino loose immediately, the State is trying to save the credibility of the police force before the opposition can turn this into a systemic indictment of the government's security culture.

Moving from the policing of the streets to the policing of the algorithm.

The Milan Prosecutor's Office has placed the Italian branch of Deliveroo under "judicial administration," a measure usually reserved for companies infiltrated by the mafia, but here applied to digital exploitation. According to *Il Sole 24 Ore*, the investigation unveiled a system where over 20,000 riders were managed not as autonomous freelancers, but as underpaid subordinates stripped of basic rights. The data is damning: *Agie* and *Fanpage* highlight testimonies of riders cycling 150 kilometers a day for a net pay of roughly three euros per delivery, effectively ninety percent below the national poverty threshold.

The spin on this story highlights the friction between innovation and regulation. *Il Fatto Quotidiano* frames this as the inevitable outcome of a neoliberal model that the government has failed to regulate, explicitly calling it "digital feudalism." Conversely, the business-friendly pages of *Il Giornale* are quieter on the moral outrage but acknowledge the legal peril, noting that this investigation is expanding to other giants like McDonald’s and Esselunga. The consequence is clear: the "Wild West" era of the gig economy in Italy is hitting a judicial wall. With the Trump administration's tariffs already squeezing the traditional export economy, Italy can ill afford a domestic labor crisis in the service sector, yet the courts seem determined to force a restructuring of how digital labor is compensated.

Turning to the South, where the diagnosis for the public health sector is far more terminal than a simple lack of funds.

In a story that reads like a script from a crime drama, DDA prosecutors have uncovered that the Camorra’s Clan Contini had essentially annexed the San Giovanni Bosco hospital in Naples. *La Stampa* and *Adnkronos* report that the clan didn't just infiltrate the facility; they ran it. They managed the bar, the cleaning services, and crucially, the ambulances, using the hospital as a logistics hub for illicit activities and insurance fraud. Fake medical certificates were issued to get affiliates out of prison, and doctors were allegedly coerced or bribed into compliance.

The political fallout is heavy. *Libero* uses the arrests to underscore a narrative of southern institutional failure, suggesting that pouring more EU recovery funds into such a system is like pouring water into a sieve. However, *La Repubblica* focuses on the human cost, noting that while the politicians in Rome debate the "Autonomia Differenziata" (regional autonomy), the reality on the ground is that the State has already ceded sovereignty in critical infrastructure to organized crime. It is a stark reminder that in parts of the country, the "Machine" of the state has been entirely replaced by the "Machine" of the clan.

But before we go, let’s return briefly to the Italian Riviera, where the numbers are down but the neurons are firing.

Despite the ratings flop for Carlo Conti’s debut, which saw a significant dip in share compared to the Amadeus era, *AGI* reports on a fascinating study regarding what happens to the Italian brain during Sanremo. Neurologist Paolo Maria Rossini explains that the Festival activates a "neural network" involving memory, emotion, and language that few other events can trigger. It turns out that hate-watching the Festival is biologically indistinguishable from loving it—your brain is engaged either way. And perhaps the most poignant moment of that engagement came from 105-year-old Gianna Pratesi. Standing on the Ariston stage, she recounted voting in 1946, telling the audience that while her family was "all left-wing," the choice was simple: "No fascists, Republic." A reminder that some memories are durable enough to outlast even the longest, most exhausting song contest on earth.
