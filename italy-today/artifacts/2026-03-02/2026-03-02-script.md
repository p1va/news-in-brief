Welcome to another episode of "Italy Today", today is Monday 02 March and this episode was curated by Gemini 3 Pro.

The situation in the Middle East has escalated from a surgical strike to a regional conflagration, and the shockwaves are already rattling windows here in Rome. While Israeli forces launch ground incursions into Lebanon and American jets engage over Kuwait, the real nightmare for Palazzo Chigi is economic: Qatar Energy has halted LNG production. Closer to home, the Italian economy misses its deficit target, leaving us tethered to Brussels' leash for another year, and a horrific car chase in the capital has left an entire family dead. Meanwhile, the investigation into the Milan tram disaster takes a darker, more confused turn.

Our lead story today is the cost of war, both political and economic.

If you thought the killing of Ayatollah Khamenei was the end of the story, today’s markets beg to differ. The conflict has officially widened, with *Corriere della Sera* reporting active engagements involving Hezbollah in Lebanon and US forces in Kuwait. But the headline that should actually worry you isn't about missiles, but methane. *La Repubblica* and *La Stampa* lead with the news that Qatar Energy has suspended LNG production due to security risks in the Gulf. This is the "high consequence" event we fear: Italy relies on Qatar for nearly 45% of its liquefied natural gas. *Il Sole 24 Ore* warns bluntly that if this blockade holds, we are looking at an immediate spike in energy bills and a potential industrial slowdown, just as the European economy was trying to find its footing.

Against this backdrop of global peril, the Italian political class is embroiled in a controversy that feels almost parochial. Defense Minister Guido Crosetto has managed to return to Rome from Dubai, but the optics are terrible. *Fanpage* and *Il Fatto Quotidiano* are eviscerating the Minister, framing his presence in a luxury holiday destination on the eve of a predictable war as a dereliction of duty. They argue that while the Gulf was burning, Italy’s defense chief was essentially a tourist scrambling for a seat on a plane.

The government's defense, mounted vigorously by *Il Giornale* and *Libero*, is that the escalation was an "intelligence failure" shared by the entire EU, not just Crosetto. *Il Giornale* emphasizes the Minister’s "gesture of responsibility," noting that he paid triple the cost of the state flight out of his own pocket to avoid burdening the taxpayer. It is a bizarre defense—a Minister paying a premium to his own government to save face—but it highlights the extreme sensitivity of Meloni’s team to accusations of privilege. The Prime Minister herself has had to intervene, stating dryly that Crosetto "never stopped working," a line that *La Stampa* interprets as a cold, necessary shield for a Minister who has become a liability.

Turning to the economy, the numbers are in, and they are not what Giancarlo Giorgetti wanted to see.

The "adult in the room" narrative took a hit today as ISTAT released the preliminary deficit figures for 2025. The magic number was 3.0%—the threshold to exit the EU’s excessive deficit procedure. The actual number is 3.1%. As *Il Sole 24 Ore* explains, that 0.1% miss is a political canyon. It means Italy remains under Brussels' microscope, limiting the government’s ability to spend on healthcare or tax cuts.

The spin war is immediate and predictable. The Ministry of Economy is laying the blame entirely at the feet of the past. *Libero* and government sources are screaming "Superbonus," arguing that the tail end of the Five Star Movement’s construction credits is still poisoning the well. However, the opposition isn't buying it. Giuseppe Conte, cited in *Il Fatto*, calls these "puerile excuses," pointing out that with GDP growth stuck at a meager 0.5%, the government’s own engine is stalling. The takeaway here is structural: the "economic miracle" Meloni promised is bogged down in stagnation, and the government is running out of alibis.

Moving from the balance sheets to the tracks, the investigation into the Milan tram disaster has shifted from an accident inquiry to a criminal probe.

The driver of the tram that crashed into a building last Friday is now formally under investigation for "railway disaster" and homicide. *La Repubblica* reports that initial findings suggest the tram was traveling at excessive speed, violently ruling out the initial theories of a simple technical failure. But the most grotesque element of this story emerged today regarding the victims.

For days, we were told that one of the deceased was a homeless man named Karim Tourè. Today, prosecutors revealed a tragic case of mistaken identity. The victim was actually Johnson Okon Lucky, a 49-year-old Nigerian national. *Il Giorno* and *Corriere della Sera* report that authorities are still trying to locate his family. It is a grim footnote that speaks volumes about the invisibility of certain lives in our metropolises—a man dies in a public disaster, and for three days, the state buries the wrong name.

But before we go, we mark the end of an era in Italian pop culture.

Alfonso Signorini, the high priest of Italian gossip, has announced he is leaving the direction of *Chi* magazine. For twenty years, *Chi* was not just a tabloid; under the Berlusconi empire, it was a political instrument that humanized the powerful and scandalized the opposition. *Il Giornale* salutes him as a revolutionary of the genre, while *Il Fatto* sarcastically notes his exit comes just as he denies any link to the latest Fabrizio Corona scandal. Regardless of the motive, his departure signals that the "Berlusconismo" aesthetic—that specific blend of power, plastic surgery, and paparazzi—is finally fading into history.
