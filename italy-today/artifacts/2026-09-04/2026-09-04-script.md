Our lead story today takes us to Bari, where Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni officially celebrated her government becoming the longest-lasting in the history of the Italian Republic. Addressing a heavily secured rally at the port, Meloni framed this 1,413-day milestone not just as a political victory, but as a structural reform in itself. She told the crowd that institutional stability is a shield, teasing upcoming measures on family bonuses and national security, while declaring to her detractors that she has absolutely no intention of backing down.

However, the political spin on this celebration is heavily fractured. The conservative press, including Libero and Il Giornale, is treating the event as a triumph, focusing on the roaring crowds and the Prime Minister’s defiant rhetoric. They paint a picture of a unified center-right family marching confidently into the second half of its mandate. But read the opposition press, and you get a completely different narrative. Il Manifesto points out that both the Lega and Forza Italia leadership effectively snubbed the event, leaving Meloni to celebrate almost entirely alone. Meanwhile, La Repubblica highlights the glaring dissonance of the moment: as Meloni boasts of stability, her coalition is dealing with a surge in fuel prices and a formal military probe into the rising power of General Roberto Vannacci's internal faction. The takeaway is that the administration is successfully using the mere fact of its survival as a powerful marketing tool. But beneath the celebratory lights in Puglia, the opposition is betting that institutional longevity won't mask the underlying economic friction and the growing ideological divides within the right-wing coalition.

Moving from political celebrations to harsh economic realities, a massive blow has struck the industrial south. Letters of dismissal have officially been sent to 2,500 workers in the satellite industries surrounding the former ILVA steel plant in Taranto. The local business association, Aigi, called the collective layoffs an inevitable consequence of the shutdown of the plant's hot area and a total lack of government planning. While union leaders are demanding an urgent meeting in Rome, the progressive wing is already weaponizing the crisis, with Democratic Party leader Elly Schlein accusing the government of wasting time on self-celebration while the working class loses its livelihood.

Turning to the housing crisis, a new directive from Brussels is about to send shockwaves through Italy's real estate market. The European Union has drafted the "Affordable Housing Act," which introduces a continent-wide metric to define areas under severe housing stress. According to the draft, if it takes more than eight years of average income to buy a house in a specific municipality, local mayors will be granted the power to strictly limit or even ban short-term rental licenses. While housing rights advocates are cheering the move as a necessary weapon against over-tourism in cities like Florence, Rome, and Milan, property owner associations like Aigab—heavily backed by the Lega—are furious, warning that millions of tourist beds are now at risk of being wiped out by EU bureaucracy.
