We begin with the blitz. The Italian gold rush continues at the Winter Games, with Andrea Vötter and Marion Oberhofer making history by winning the women's doubles luge, a feat *Corriere della Sera* hails as a masterpiece of precision on the home track, prompting a personal congratulatory call from President Mattarella. In the corporate world, the legal noose tightens around the Agnelli family heir; *Il Sole 24 Ore* reports that the preliminary investigations judge has rejected John Elkann’s request for "probation" regarding the inheritance tax fraud case, meaning the files go back to the Turin prosecutors and a trial looks increasingly likely. Meanwhile, the investigation into the New Year’s massacre at Crans-Montana is turning up grim details, with *La Stampa* revealing that the "Constellation" club had unchecked ventilation systems and blocked exits, confirming suspicions of gross negligence. Finally, in a tragic echo from across the Atlantic, the Italian government has expressed formal condolences to Canada following a school shooting in British Columbia that left nine dead, a story dominating the foreign pages of *La Repubblica* today.

The Lead story today is the government’s double-down on border control, as the Council of Ministers approves a new, harder-line Migrant Decree.

Just twenty-four hours after the European Parliament greenlit the "Safe Countries" regulation, Giorgia Meloni’s cabinet has moved to domesticate that victory into strict Italian law. As detailed by *Il Giornale* and *Adnkronos*, the new decree introduces a mechanism that functions as a "administrative naval blockade." The law imposes fines of up to 50,000 euros for NGOs or vessels that violate orders to stay out of Italian waters, and crucially, it drastically simplifies the deportation process for migrants usually resident in those designated "safe" countries. Furthermore, the decree criminalizes "passive resistance" inside the Repatriation Centers (CPRs), meaning migrants who protest their detention can now face jail time.

The media narrative is split down a sharp ideological fault line. The government-friendly press, led by *Libero*, frames this as the end of the "taxi service" in the Mediterranean, emphasizing that Europe has finally woken up to the Italian model. They argue that without the threat of immediate deportation, the asylum system is paralyzed. Conversely, the opposition press is sounding the alarm on constitutional grounds. *Fanpage* and *La Repubblica* focus on the "criminalization of despair," arguing that penalizing protests inside detention centers transforms administrative holding cells into jagged prisons without due process. They quote jurists who claim the "naval blockade by fine" violates international maritime law, setting the stage for yet another clash between the Executive branch and the Judiciary.

Turning to the diplomatic stage, a storm has erupted involving the UN, France, and an Italian official.

Francesca Albanese, the UN Special Rapporteur for the Palestinian territories, is facing a concerted diplomatic offensive demanding her resignation. The spark, as reported by *La Stampa* and *ANSA*, was a speech in which she allegedly referred to Israel as a "common enemy of humanity." While Albanese claims she was misquoted and was referring to the "system of apartheid," the reaction has been swift. France’s Foreign Minister has formally requested her removal, calling the rhetoric outrageous. The split here is visceral: *Il Foglio* and the Jewish communities accuse her of barely veiled antisemitism masked as human rights advocacy, while *Il Fatto Quotidiano* defends her as a truth-teller being targeted by a "lobby" that refuses to acknowledge the reality on the ground in Gaza. It is a diplomatic headache for Rome, which must navigate its loyalty to the UN system against its strategic alignment with Paris and Tel Aviv.

Meanwhile, back in the Chamber of Deputies, the government survived a vote of confidence that exposed the fragility of its right flank.

To avoid an ambush on the decree sending weapons to Ukraine, the government was forced to call a confidence vote. The spotlight was entirely on General Roberto Vannacci and his "Futuro Nazionale" loyalists. In a maneuver that *Corriere della Sera* describes as "political acrobatics," Vannacci’s deputies voted *yes* to the confidence (keeping the government alive) but declared they would vote *no* to the actual decree on weapons. *Libero* and the right-wing press are trying to downplay this as a minor internal debate, but political analysts in *La Repubblica* see it differently: Vannacci is carving out a "MAGA-style" niche within the coalition, positioning himself as the pacifist alternative to Meloni’s Atlanticism, ready to intercept the weary electorate when the time comes.

But before we go, we leave the cold tracks of the Olympics for the warm, velvet salons of Cortina’s hotels.

While athletes are hurling themselves down ice chutes at 100 kilometers per hour, *Corriere della Sera* reports that the *real* sport in Cortina is happening around the card tables. The article paints a delicious picture of the Roman and Milanese establishment, who have descended on the Dolomites not to watch the skiing, but to engage in fierce tournaments of "Burraco"—a card game beloved by the Italian bourgeoisie. Between the fur coats and the spritz, the "Burraco Olimpico" has apparently generated more tension and rivalry among the visiting VIPs than the actual medal count. It serves as a reminder that in Italy, the social event is always more important than the sporting one.
