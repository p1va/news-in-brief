Kicking off with the global headlines, a historic and highly controversial summit is unfolding in Shenzhen, where US President Donald Trump has been personally embraced by Xi Jinping alongside Vladimir Putin. With the European Union visibly sidelined and still reeling from the US tariffs imposed last year, the Italian press is already dubbing this trilateral meeting a "New Yalta" that could redraw global trade and security alliances. Back in Rome, Defense Minister Guido Crosetto addressed Parliament on the escalating threat of hybrid warfare, requesting 6 billion euros to hire 5,000 cybersecurity specialists. This follows the wave of fake bomb emails that evacuated hundreds of Italian schools; investigators have now traced the alias "Mikhail Andropov" to an American citizen in Phoenix, though the FBI denies he actually sent the threats. 

In media news, the internal war at state broadcaster RAI has escalated, with Sigfrido Ranucci, the prominent anchor of the investigative program "Report," officially suspended for ten days over alleged disciplinary breaches, a move critics are calling political retaliation. Meanwhile, the Rome prosecutor's office has opened an investigation into "Futuro Nazionale," the new political party founded by controversial right-wing General Roberto Vannacci, to determine if the movement violates the Scelba Law against reforming the fascist party. And finally, following up on yesterday's canceled Russian film festival at Rome's Barberini cinema, the event has quickly found a new home—unsurprisingly, it will now be hosted directly at the official Casa Russa in the capital.

Turning to our main story, the political storm over the 2027 budget law is intensifying. Following up on yesterday's report regarding the government's push for a sweeping building amnesty, or *condono edilizio*, the stakes have just been raised. Fratelli d'Italia has introduced a new package of amendments that would extend this amnesty to cover seismic irregularities in earthquake-prone zones and halt demolition orders for illegal structures in regions like Campania. 

The framing of this maneuver is entirely split. On the right, the government insists this is not a free pass for criminals, but a pragmatic administrative clearing-house. The ruling coalition argues that freezing minor demolition orders and waiving certain seismic project filings will unclog local courts and allow families to finally normalize their homes without facing criminal prosecution. However, the opposition and progressive outlets are outraged. *La Repubblica* and *Il Manifesto* are aggressively defining this as a "maxi-amnesty" that rewards environmental destruction and illegal developers. They argue that slipping a pardon for missing earthquake-safety permits into a budget law is a dangerous capitulation that puts citizens' lives at risk simply to secure political consensus. 

The structural takeaway is that Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni is walking a very fine line. By using the budget to deregulate the housing sector, her government is betting that the economic and electoral stimulus of freeing up the real estate market will outweigh the severe backlash over institutionalizing illegality. 

Moving from the ruling majority to the opposition, the center-left Democratic Party has suffered a highly symbolic fracture. Emanuele Fiano, a prominent Jewish politician and historical figure within the PD, announced on live television that he is leaving the party. The rupture was triggered by the PD's refusal to support a new bill combating antisemitism, which the left-wing opposition argued was a political bludgeon wielded by the right. Fiano stated that the party's failure to back the bill crossed an "insurmountable limit" regarding his identity. This exit exposes a deep and unresolved structural divide within Elly Schlein's progressive coalition, which continues to struggle with its internal positioning on Israel, the Middle East, and institutional antisemitism.

On the institutional and economic front, Prime Minister Meloni is making pragmatic moves on the European chessboard. Yesterday at Palazzo Chigi, Meloni hosted Pablo Hernández de Cos, the former Governor of the Bank of Spain and the leading candidate for the presidency of the European Central Bank. *La Stampa* notes the political irony here: Meloni is effectively giving Italy's blessing to a candidate championed by her ideological rival, Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez. It is a clear signal that when it comes to the macroeconomic stability of the Eurozone, Rome is prioritizing technical competence and southern European alliances over partisan politics. Domestically, the government has also moved to fill a crucial institutional vacuum, officially nominating law professor Remo Morzenti as the new president of the National Anti-Corruption Authority, ANAC. This smooths over a recent political embarrassment after the League's initial candidate was forced to withdraw amidst fierce polemics. 

But before we go, a quiet revolution is coming to the digital wallets of millions of Italians. As reported by *Il Sole 24 Ore*, Poste Italiane has just struck a historic agreement with BancomatPay. Starting in April 2027, millions of Italians who rely on the postal service for their primary banking will finally be able to send and receive money instantly using just a phone number, bypassing the need for cumbersome IBAN codes. It might sound like a minor tech update, but in a country where the post office remains the financial backbone for the elderly and rural communities, it is a massive structural leap toward finally bridging Italy's digital divide.
