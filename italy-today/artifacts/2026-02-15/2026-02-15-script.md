Welcome to another episode of "Italy Today", today is Sunday 15 and this episode was curated by Gemini 3 Pro.

We begin with the Blitz.

The chaos on the national railway network continues to grip the peninsula, as new acts of sabotage hit the Rome-Napoli and Rome-Florence high-speed lines this morning. While *La Repubblica* reports resignation among passengers facing delays of over two hours, Deputy Premier Matteo Salvini has escalated his rhetoric, defining these acts not merely as vandalism but as "criminal boycotts" orchestrated by the anarchist galaxy to disrupt the country during the Olympics.

Turning to the geopolitical stage, the Munich Security Conference is ending on a note of fragile reassurance. *Il Giornale* highlights the "sigh of relief" in the room as US Senator Marco Rubio stated that America and Europe are "inextricably linked," correcting the isolationist tone set by JD Vance last year. However, *La Stampa* notes that European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen remains wary, insisting that Europe must push for defense autonomy regardless of Washington’s mood.

In Verona, a new social fracture is opening over policing. Senator Ilaria Cucchi has released a graphic video showing the final moments of Moussa Diarra, the migrant killed by a police officer at the Verona station. While the right-wing press defends the officer’s claim of self-defense, *Fanpage* and the left-wing opposition are using the footage—which reportedly shows the victim holding a simple piece of cutlery rather than a weapon—to challenge the narrative, with Cucchi pointedly remarking, "We are not in Minneapolis."

And finally, tragedy strikes the Alps at the height of the season. An avalanche in Val Veny, above Courmayeur, has claimed the lives of two skiers, with a third in critical condition. *Il Messaggero* reports they were skiing off-piste in the "Ves" canal, a grim reminder from nature just as the eyes of the world are fixed on the meticulously groomed slopes of the Olympics.

But the story dominating the headlines today is a horrifying descent into medical negligence that has shocked the nation: the case of the "burnt heart."

Following up on the tragedy we reported earlier, the details emerging today regarding the failed transplant of a two-year-old child in Naples have shifted from sad to scandalous. According to *Corriere della Sera* and *Il Mattino*, the NAS carabinieri investigations suggest a catastrophic error in the logistical chain: the donor heart did not travel in a medical-grade device, but in a "common plastic box" packed with dry ice. This crude method caused the organ to freeze and necrotize—essentially "burning" it—rendering it useless before it even reached the operating table.

The institutional fallout is now total warfare between Italy’s medical giants. As we noted yesterday, the Bambino Gesù hospital in Rome has declared the child "no longer transplantable," signaling the end of hope. However, *La Repubblica* reports that the Monaldi hospital in Naples is refusing to accept this verdict, keeping the child at the top of the national emergency list in a desperate bid to save face and life. The narrative split is stark: *Il Giornale* focuses on the technical incompetence of the transport, calling it a "genesis of a disaster," while the local press in Naples circles the wagons, framing Rome's diagnosis as a dismissal of Southern healthcare capabilities. The result is a family trapped between a freezing plastic box and a bureaucratic wall.

Moving from the operating room to a constitutional clash, the war over the judiciary has reached a fever pitch.

Justice Minister Carlo Nordio has thrown a grenade into the debate over the separation of careers, explicitly defining the current appointment system within the CSM—the self-governing body of the judiciary—as "para-mafia." Speaking to newspapers in the northeast, Nordio claimed that the current logic of currents and favors creates a system where prosecutors must find a "godfather" to advance. *Libero* and *La Verità* are championing this aggressive stance, positioning the upcoming referendum on justice not just as a technical reform, but as a moral cleansing of a corrupt "caste."

The backlash, however, has been ferocious. *La Stampa* reports the indignation of the National Association of Magistrates (ANM), who accuse the Minister of insulting the memory of judges killed by the actual mafia. The opposition, led by Elly Schlein and Giuseppe Conte, is demanding that Prime Minister Meloni distance herself from Nordio's "subversive" language. We are witnessing a dangerous escalation: the government is no longer just criticizing judicial overreach; it is delegitimizing the very structure of the judiciary, using the language of organized crime to describe the state’s own servants.

Turning to a rare moment of national unity, albeit one born on the snow, Italy has unintentionally broken a historical record.

Despite the sabotage on the rails and the political infighting, the Milan-Cortina Olympics have delivered a "Super Sunday." With Federica Brignone’s gold in the Giant Slalom and Lisa Vittozzi’s historic win in the Biathlon, Italy has surpassed its all-time winter medal record set in Lillehammer in 1994. *La Gazzetta* and *Corriere* celebrate this as the triumph of "The Sisters of Italy," noting that while the country's infrastructure crumbles under arson attacks and bad management, its individual talent remains world-class. It is a perfect, paradoxical snapshot of the country: a state that struggles to run trains or transport organs, yet produces individuals who can outpace the world.

But before we go, a final note on love and erosion.

On this Valentine's weekend, nature has broken up with one of Italy's most romantic landmarks. The famous "Arco degli Innamorati" (Arch of Lovers) at Torre Sant’Andrea in Salento has collapsed into the sea. Battered by the violent "Cyclone Oriana," the limestone bridge simply dissolved overnight. Local mayors call it a blow to tourism, but perhaps there is a poetic lesson here: even rock requires maintenance, and nothing—not monuments, not infrastructure, and certainly not love—stands forever against the storm.
