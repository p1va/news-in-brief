We begin with the Blitz.

Italy is waking up to a gold rush and a diplomatic headache. At the Milano-Cortina Olympics, the nation is celebrating a historic double triumph: Federica Brignone’s gold in the Super-G, described by *Il Giornale* as a masterpiece born of suffering, and short-track legend Arianna Fontana equaling the all-time Italian Olympic medal record. But the ice is thin elsewhere: the International Olympic Committee has upheld the ban on a Ukrainian skeleton athlete’s helmet, which depicted compatriots killed in the war—a move Kiev calls "hypocrisy," while officials cite the ban on political symbols.

Meanwhile, Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni is in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, pushing her flagship "Mattei Plan." While *La Repubblica* notes her conspicuous absence from the Munich Security Conference to attend this summit, government sources via *Adnkronos* frame this as Italy cementing its role as the "privileged bridge" between Europe and Africa.

And finally, a chilling snapshot of Italian youth. A new Save the Children report reveals that one in four Italian adolescents has suffered violence in a relationship, with one in three being geolocated by their partner against their will—a statistic *La Stampa* calls the normalization of control.

But the story gripping the nation today is a medical nightmare that bridges the North and the South.

We are talking about the tragedy of the "burnt heart." A two-year-old child at the Monaldi hospital in Naples is fighting for his life after receiving a donor heart that was allegedly damaged—or "burned"—during transport from Bolzano. The timeline is harrowing: the organ was explanted in the far North, transported to Naples, and implanted, only for surgeons to discover the tissue was necrotized, likely due to improper thermal preservation. The child is now surviving solely on mechanical support.

The spin on this story reveals the raw nerves of Italy's healthcare system. *La Repubblica* and *La Stampa* focus on the agony of the family and the "chain of errors," highlighting the seizure of the transport box by the Carabinieri and the potential negligence of six medical professionals now under investigation. They paint a picture of a logistical failure that has doomed a toddler. Conversely, the government-aligned press like *Il Giornale*, while acknowledging the horror, emphasizes the immediate response: Health Minister Schillaci has dispatched inspectors to both Bolzano and Naples, and the Ministry has placed the child at the absolute top of the national and international transplant lists in a desperate race against time.

The synthesis here goes beyond malpractice. It is a structural stress test of the national transplant network. As the family lawyer tells *Corriere della Sera*, they are now seeking a third-party opinion from the Bambino Gesù hospital in Rome, effectively losing faith in the local institution. It is a story that tragically illustrates the fragility of the "machine" when logistics fail medicine.

Moving from the operating theater to the airport terminal, the Olympics are facing a different kind of blockage.

Deputy Prime Minister Matteo Salvini has decided to play hardball with the trade unions. With air transport strikes planned for February 16 and March 7—dates that overlap with the Olympic and Paralympic window—the Minister of Transport has signed a "precettazione," a legal order forcing workers to stay on the job.

The narrative divide here is sharp. *Il Giornale* and *Libero* frame this as a necessary defense of the national image, quoting Salvini’s rationale that Italy cannot allow a "planetary event" watched by two billion people to be held hostage by labor disputes. It is the "country that works" versus the "party of No." However, the opposition press and labor unions, particularly the CGIL, view this through a different lens. They see it as an authoritarian erosion of the right to strike, using the Games as a convenient shield to suppress legitimate demands over expired contracts. It’s a classic Italian standoff: the right to mobility versus the right to protest, with the five Olympic rings raising the stakes.

Turning to the judiciary, the temperature has reached a boiling point between the government and one of Italy’s most famous prosecutors.

Nicola Gratteri, the Prosecutor of Naples, has set off a political firestorm with comments regarding the upcoming referendum on the separation of careers for judges and prosecutors. In an interview, Gratteri declared that those voting "Yes" to the reform include "deviant freemasons, defendants, and suspects," effectively categorizing supporters of the government-backed reform as acting in bad faith or self-interest.

The backlash was instant. *La Verità* and *Il Giornale* have launched a full-scale offensive, with Justice Minister Carlo Nordio expressing "bewilderment" and suggesting that perhaps magistrates need "psycho-aptitude tests," implying Gratteri is out of control. The League has threatened lawsuits. Conversely, *Il Fatto Quotidiano* and *La Repubblica* are rallying around Gratteri, framing his outburst as the blunt truth of a frontline anti-mafia fighter warning against a reform that would weaken the judiciary's independence. This isn't just a war of words; it’s the opening salvo of a referendum campaign that promises to be brutal.

But before we go, a story that proves you are truly what you eat.

We return to the Olympics for a bizarre twist in the doping saga of biathlete Rebecca Passler. She had been suspended for testing positive for a banned substance, Letrozole, but has now been fully reinstated just in time to race. The defense that cleared her? Nutella. It turns out Passler likely ingested the substance by sharing a jar of the hazelnut spread—and the same spoon—with her mother, who was taking medication for a serious illness unbeknownst to her daughter. It is a ruling that combines family drama, medical details, and a national confectionery icon in a way that could only happen in Italy.
