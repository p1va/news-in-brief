That brings us to our lead story. The government has just published its Public Finance Document, the blueprint for the upcoming budget, and it reveals a massive pivot in state spending. Out of the 29-billion-euro extra-deficit allowance requested from the European Union, the government is dramatically slashing its projected defense spending. According to the framework, the military will receive about 7 billion euros less than originally planned over the next two years. The reason? The government is bracing for a severe energy shock in 2027 and is redirecting those funds to shield families, students, and businesses from skyrocketing utility bills.

The political spin on these military cuts is entirely dependent on where you look. The right-wing daily *La Verità* defends Economy Minister Giancarlo Giorgetti, framing him as a pragmatist who is rightly prioritizing the wallets of Italian families over weapons in a highly complex European economic environment. On the exact opposite end of the spectrum, the communist *Il Manifesto* is accusing the government of an accounting trick. They argue that Rome is merely halving—not cancelling—the 14 billion euros earmarked for rearmament, hiding a massive military buildup behind a facade of energy subsidies. 

The takeaway here is that Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni is attempting a near-impossible balancing act. She is caught between President Donald Trump's relentless, tariff-backed pressure on NATO members to aggressively boost defense spending, and a looming domestic energy crisis that threatens her working-class base. By cutting defense to subsidize energy, she is prioritizing domestic survival, but she risks drawing the geopolitical ire of Washington.

Moving from the state budget to the banking sector, a massive battle for the future of Italian finance is escalating. Intesa Sanpaolo has unexpectedly raised its cash offer to acquire Monte dei Paschi di Siena by 25 cents per share, bringing the total valuation of the takeover to an eye-watering 31.4 billion euros. As reported by *Il Sole 24 Ore*, this aggressive move comes with a strict ultimatum: Intesa has declared it will walk away entirely if MPS shareholders approve a competing internal plan at their assembly later this month. It is a high-stakes poker game that will ultimately determine the consolidation of the European financial market and the fate of Italy's oldest bank.

Turning to a massive breach of privacy in the healthcare system, a sleeper story with widespread implications has just hit the regulatory wires. The national privacy guarantor has fined the data analytics firm Iqvia 7 million euros. Unnoticed by much of the mainstream press, this breach exposed the highly sensitive, unencrypted medical data of over one million Italian patients across 800 medical practices. Everything from birth dates to diagnoses, prescriptions, and location data was left exposed. It is a stark wake-up call about the fragile cybersecurity infrastructure underpinning the national health service, and a reminder of how easily our most intimate data can slip through the cracks.

For the first time since 1977, October 4th—the feast day of Saint Francis of Assisi, Italy’s patron saint—has been officially reinstated as a national holiday. Because the date falls on a Sunday this year, labor laws dictate that workers who have the day off will receive a "non-enjoyed holiday" bonus in their October salaries, worth roughly 40 to 50 euros depending on your contract.
