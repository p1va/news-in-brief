But the main story today is a direct follow-up to the brewing economic storm we covered yesterday. With diesel prices hitting a record 2.23 euros per liter and the threat of a nationwide trucker strike looming, the government needed a release valve. Today, they found one not through legislation, but through corporate intervention. Energy giant Eni announced a surprise, voluntary 30-day price cap, freezing diesel at 2.19 euros and gasoline at 1.99 euros at the pump, starting this Monday. 

The political framing of this move is incredibly split. The Meloni government and conservative allies are celebrating this as a patriotic intervention. Palazzo Chigi immediately released a statement thanking Eni for its "praiseworthy act of attention to Italy," framing the move as a crucial shield for Italian families against a difficult international market. However, the opposition, alongside progressive outlets like La Repubblica and Il Fatto Quotidiano, are blasting the move as a temporary PR stunt. They point out that the government is simultaneously reducing its own state-sponsored discount on fuel excises, effectively outsourcing its economic policy to a state-controlled corporation while dodging the structural issue of high taxes.

The ultimate takeaway here is how the Meloni administration is increasingly leveraging state-backed corporate behemoths to defuse domestic social crises, blurring the line between corporate strategy and national economic policy to keep the country moving.

Bringing it back to the Italian economy, a new battle line has been drawn over local taxes. The national tax collection agency released the list of roughly 1,500 municipalities that have opted into the government's "Rottamazione-quinquies"—a sweeping amnesty program allowing citizens to scrap the interest and penalties on unpaid local taxes like the IMU property tax, garbage fees, and traffic tickets. The list exposes a stark geographical and political divide. Major central and southern cities like Rome, Naples, and Bari have eagerly signed on, desperate to clear billions in uncollectable, dead-weight debt from their ledgers. Conversely, much of the industrial North, including Milan, has flatly rejected the amnesty. While government-aligned forces argue this is pragmatic housekeeping to help struggling citizens, the center-left opposition frames it as a slap in the face to honest taxpayers and a state-sanctioned reward for chronic tax evasion.
