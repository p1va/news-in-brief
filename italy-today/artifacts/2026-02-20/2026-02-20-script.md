Welcome to another episode of "Italy Today", today is Friday 20 and this episode was curated by Gemini 3 Pro.

We begin with the Blitz.

The tragedy at the Monaldi Hospital in Naples is reaching its heartbreaking conclusion. Following the revelation that a donor heart arrived encased in ice—rendering it unusable—the lawyers for the family of little Domenico announced on live television that the two-year-old has been moved to palliative care. The medical team and the family have agreed to stop life-sustaining treatments to end his suffering, a decision the legal team emphasized is not euthanasia, but a rejection of "therapeutic obstinacy."

In Rome, Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni has attempted to put the brakes on the institutional crisis she helped ignite. After President Mattarella sent a subtle but firm signal regarding the independence of the judiciary, Meloni appeared on SkyTG24 to dial down the rhetoric, stating that the upcoming referendum on the separation of judicial careers is "not a vote on me" and rejecting what she called a "mud-slinging contest."

However, the ceasefire is only partial. While the Premier softens her tone, Interior Minister Matteo Piantedosi is holding the line on the migration front. Responding to the Palermo court ruling that ordered the state to pay damages to the NGO Sea Watch, Piantedosi announced this morning that the Viminale will appeal the sentence, insisting that the government’s security policies are yielding results despite the judicial setbacks.

In Milan, the "Rogoredo shooting" case has taken a dark turn for the police force. During interrogations, four officers have effectively turned on their colleague, Carmelo Cinturrino, who is under investigation for killing a drug dealer. *Corriere della Sera* reports that the officers testified that Cinturrino lied about calling for immediate medical aid—waiting 23 minutes—and described him as a "fanatic" who managed the scene alone, bolstering the prosecution's theory of a cover-up.

And finally, a changing of the guard at State broadcaster Rai. Following the disastrous coverage of the Olympic opening ceremony and a revolt by the newsroom, sources confirm that Rai Sport director Paolo Petrecca will officially resign after the Milano-Cortina games conclude, leaving a power vacuum in one of the most sensitive seats in public television.

The lead story today comes not from Rome, but from Washington, where a gavel strike in the US Supreme Court has sent shockwaves through the Italian economy.

In a stunning rebuke to the White House, the US Supreme Court has ruled 6-3 that President Donald Trump did not have the authority to unilaterally impose the sweeping tariffs that have burdened global trade since 2025. The ruling declares the tariffs invalid, a decision that *Il Sole 24 Ore* reports has triggered a wave of cautious relief among Italian exporters, from the fashion houses of Milan to the prosecco vineyards of Veneto. For an economy that lives and dies by its ability to sell "Made in Italy" abroad, the prospect of these trade barriers vanishing is a lifeline.

However, the reaction in Rome is a study in nervous pragmatism. Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani, trying to maintain the government's close alignment with the Trump administration, downplayed the shock, telling *ANSA* that the decision was "not unexpected" and suggesting that business will continue as usual. But the press tells a different story. *La Repubblica* characterizes the ruling as a harsh reminder to the Trump White House that the President is "not a King," while financial analysts warn that the trade war is far from over. Trump has already threatened a "Plan B"—invoking different national security laws to reinstall a 10% global tariff, a move that would restart the chaos.

The synthesis here is delicate. While the political opposition in Italy views the Supreme Court ruling as a victory for the rule of law against populism, the Meloni government is walking on eggshells. They cannot celebrate a defeat for Trump—their key ideological ally—but they desperately need the economic relief that this defeat provides. As *Il Foglio* notes, the government is hoping for the best but preparing for a "wounded beast" reaction from Washington that could result in even more unpredictable economic retaliation.

Moving from the complexities of geopolitics to the vindication of science in the Italian South.

Currently, *Il Foglio* highlights a quiet but historic ruling from the Tribunal of Bari that has officially archived the investigations against scientists accused of causing the Xylella bacterial outbreak. For years, conspiracy theories championed by parts of the Five Star Movement and local populists claimed that researchers were complicit in spreading the disease that decimated millions of olive trees in Puglia. Now, the court has ruled definitively: there was no plot, only a desperate attempt by science to stop a plague, hindered by politics. It is a stark indictment of a decade where superstition was allowed to stall agricultural policy, costing the region its landscape and its economy.

Meanwhile, a scandal of a very different nature is rocking the rigid world of Italian bureaucracy.

A breach at the National Council of Notaries has exposed the informal and potentially discriminatory criteria used to judge candidates for one of the country’s most elite professions. *Corriere della Sera* and *Il Fatto Quotidiano* report that a spreadsheet was accidentally published online containing internal notes on candidates who had passed the written exam. Alongside their grades were comments like "Carina" (cute), "Fenomeno" (phenomenon), and worryingly, "Graziato" (pardoned/let off easy). For a selection process that claims absolute meritocracy, the leak reinforces the darkest suspicions of the Italian public: that even in the highest state exams, who you know—and how you look—might matter as much as what you know.

But before we go, a mystery worthy of a Dan Brown novel has opened up inside the Vatican.

*AGI* reports that a new scientific committee has been quietly formed to investigate a sensational claim: that Michelangelo Buonarroti did not destroy all his sketches before he died, as history has always believed. A new study suggests that roughly twenty lost sketches by the Renaissance master were hidden away by his students in a "locked room" to save them from his fury. As the world prepares to celebrate the 550th anniversary of his birth, the Vatican is launching a rigorous forensic analysis. If confirmed, it would be the art discovery of the century—a reminder that in Rome, the past is never really dead; it’s just waiting behind a locked door.
