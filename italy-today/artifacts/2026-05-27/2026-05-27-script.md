The right-wing majority has unilaterally accelerated the timeline for Italy’s new electoral law, internally dubbed the "Bignami Bis." The bill is now scheduled to hit the Chamber floor on June 26th. The proposed legislation fundamentally rewrites how Italians will elect their representatives. It eliminates the possibility of run-off elections, alters the threshold required to trigger a majority premium of seats, and makes party lists legally inadmissible unless they explicitly name a candidate for Prime Minister. 

The political framing of this acceleration is drastically split. The government-aligned Il Giornale defends the fast-track approach, framing it as a long-overdue streamlining of an inefficient voting system and emphasizing that Fratelli d'Italia remains open to minor modifications. Conversely, the opposition press is up in arms. Il Fatto Quotidiano and La Repubblica blast the move as an "unprecedented forcing" and an institutional blitz. They argue the majority is rewriting the rules of the game to permanently lock in structural advantages for the right, bypassing proper parliamentary debate to force a tailor-made system before the summer. 

The takeaway here is that Prime Minister Meloni’s coalition is aggressively moving to consolidate institutional power. By forcing a direct link between parliamentary lists and a Premier candidate, the right is effectively trying to legally mandate the bipolar, presidential-style system they have long campaigned for, daring the fragmented opposition to either unite under a single leader or face electoral irrelevance.

Moving from domestic election rules to foreign policy, a massive crack has opened within the ruling majority over the future of the European Union. The prospect of opening the first formal negotiation chapter for Ukraine’s accession to the EU has triggered an all-out clash between the coalition partners. The Lega party issued a harsh official statement declaring absolute opposition, calling Kiev’s entry an "economic and social damage of enormous proportions." Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani of Forza Italia immediately distanced himself, stating his party is entirely in favor, though he noted the Western Balkans remain a priority. La Stampa highlights this as a severe vulnerability for the Meloni government. With aggressive US tariffs squeezing the domestic economy, nationalist factions are feeling emboldened. The Lega is openly breaking ranks, serving as a stark reminder that while Meloni projects Atlanticist loyalty abroad, her coalition at home remains deeply divided on the economic and geopolitical costs of the war.

The upcoming vote in the Chamber’s authorization committee will be a massive test of the majority's willingness to shield its own from judicial scrutiny.
