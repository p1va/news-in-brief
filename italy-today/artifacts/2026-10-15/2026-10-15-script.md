Our main story tonight is the money. In the dead of night, the Italian government transmitted its 2027 Budget Plan to the European Commission and the Italian Parliament. The 23.5-billion-euro package includes tax cuts for middle-income earners and support for large families. But the shockwave hitting the financial sector today is how the government plans to pay for it: a surprise 5-billion-euro levy on banks and insurance companies.

The spin across the press reflects a divided establishment. The sovereigntist right, like La Verità, proudly champions the move, framing it as a righteous extraction of wealth from the financial elite. Interestingly, the anti-establishment left at Il Fatto Quotidiano agrees with taxing the banks, but fiercely attacks the government's priorities, pointing out that the budget allocates almost double the resources to military defense than it does to the public healthcare system. Meanwhile, the progressive La Repubblica notes that despite the populist banking squeeze, the government is still forced to implement a quiet but severe spending review across all ministries to balance the books.

The takeaway is that Prime Minister Meloni is aggressively hunting for cash to satisfy both domestic voters and strict European fiscal rules. By forcing the banking sector to cough up billions—a move the banking association openly stated today was "not negotiated"—the government is taking a populist gamble. It successfully avoids broad tax hikes on citizens, but it heavily strains relations with the financial institutions Italy relies on to buy its massive public debt.

Moving from the state budget to the reality on the ground, the national statistics agency, ISTAT, released its poverty report today, and the numbers are grim. 5.4 million people—just over nine percent of the population—are living in absolute poverty in 2025. While the overall number has stabilized compared to last year, the demographic breakdown is alarming. The incidence of poverty among factory workers is double that of the average employed person, destroying the old assumption that a full-time contract guarantees a living wage. Il Fatto Quotidiano highlights that paying rent is now the primary factor pushing Italian families over the financial edge. The data paints a picture of a fractured labor market, where inflation and the rising cost of living have far outpaced wage growth.

Turning to the institutions, President Sergio Mattarella has officially run out of patience with performative politics. Today, while signing a new law establishing a "National Day for Paper Recycling," the Head of State attached a formal letter of reprimand to Parliament. He noted that Italy now has 51 state-mandated national days, with 20 of them created just in this current legislature. Ranging from "National Kindness Day" to days honoring historical costumes, Mattarella warned that the dizzying proliferation of these observances dilutes their meaning and makes it impossible to actually celebrate them. It is a rare, polite, but sharp rebuke from the Quirinale, effectively telling lawmakers to stop passing symbolic holidays and get back to real legislation.

But before we go, a culinary culture war is brewing in the northeastern province of Pordenone. Starting this school year, the traditional pork chop and prosciutto have been quietly scrubbed from the menus of local elementary school canteens. Driven by a mix of regional health guidelines favoring white meat, and a changing demographic where up to 30 percent of students in some schools are of Muslim heritage, the canteens are pivoting to chicken curry and couscous. The local mayor is desperately trying to calm the waters, insisting that no official ban on pork was ever issued and that it is purely a nutritional choice. Still, he has promised to speak with the health authority to ensure that traditional Friulian cold cuts make a swift return to the lunch trays.
