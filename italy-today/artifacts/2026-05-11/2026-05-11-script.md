And finally, an administrative spat has escalated to the highest office in the land, bringing us to today's main story.

Following up on yesterday's report, the sudden purge at the Ministry of Culture has forced Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni to personally intervene. Minister Alessandro Giuli, who abruptly fired two top officials over a dispute involving a documentary on the murdered Italian researcher Giulio Regeni, was summoned to Palazzo Chigi today for a one-hour closed-door meeting to explain the chaos. 

The framing of this meeting depends entirely on what newspaper you read. The conservative press, led by Il Giornale and Libero, is working overtime to project absolute unity. They prominently feature official statements that Meloni offered her "full support" to Giuli, dismissing the narrative of internal fractures as baseless gossip fabricated by the media. However, the progressive opposition press sees blood in the water. La Repubblica and L'Unità describe the situation as a political "earthquake," framing Giuli's firings as a desperate attempt to assert his own independence against the hardline faction of Meloni’s Brothers of Italy party.

The undeniable takeaway here is that the Ministry of Culture, originally envisioned as the engine room for the right-wing's new cultural hegemony, is rapidly becoming a political liability. When the Prime Minister has to pause her geopolitical agenda to mediate a staff dispute over historical documentaries, it signals to the country that the coalition's internal discipline is fraying at the edges.

Moving from political friction to a very real economic crisis, the Italian industrial sector took a heavy blow today. As mentioned at the top of the show, Electrolux has announced it will slash 1,700 jobs in Italy—nearly forty percent of its national workforce—and completely shut down its manufacturing plant in the province of Ancona. While Il Fatto Quotidiano and Fanpage are amplifying the outrage of the metalworker unions, who have immediately called for an eight-hour national strike, the business press notes this is part of a broader, painful European restructuring. Caught between high energy costs, shifting supply chains, and the squeeze of the Trump administration's aggressive global tariffs, multinational manufacturers are increasingly deciding that producing lower-value goods in Italy simply no longer makes financial sense.

Turning to the domestic economy, millions of Italians preparing for tax season might soon face unexpected roadblocks. Il Sole 24 Ore reports today that the government has quietly published a decree retroactively cutting over 21 million euros in funding to the CAF—the authorized centers that assist citizens with their 730 tax returns. The unions managing these centers are sounding the alarm, warning that this sudden budget slash threatens their ability to process millions of declarations. It is a highly consequential bureaucratic snag that risks infuriating voters exactly when they are trying to comply with the state.

But before we go, prepare to say goodbye to a deeply ingrained piece of Italian childhood. Il Sole 24 Ore reports that FIFA is officially ending its nearly 60-year partnership with the historic Italian company Panini. Starting in 2031, the official World Cup collectible stickers will be produced by the American company Topps. For generations of Italians, the cry of "celo, celo, manca" while trading Panini stickers in the schoolyard has been a cultural rite of passage. While the Modena-based company will surely survive, the loss of the World Cup license marks the end of a truly iconic era.
