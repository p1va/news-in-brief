That brings us to our lead story. The ghosts of the 1992 Mafia massacres have returned to haunt the highest echelons of the Italian Republic. Gianni De Gennaro, the former head of the State Police and a historical heavyweight of Italian intelligence, is formally under investigation for misdirection—or *depistaggio*—in the probe into the Via D’Amelio bombing that killed anti-mafia judge Paolo Borsellino. Prosecutors in Caltanissetta actually requested De Gennaro’s arrest, alleging he and his inner circle pressured a mafia turncoat to withhold testimony and hid crucial documents. The preliminary judge denied the arrest warrant, but the prosecutors are already appealing the decision.

The media framing reveals a deep institutional fracture. Anti-establishment and progressive outlets like Il Fatto Quotidiano and La Repubblica are treating this as a long-overdue reckoning, focusing on the dark shadows of the deep state and the still-missing "red agenda" notebook that belonged to Borsellino. Conversely, institutional papers like Il Messaggero describe the request to arrest a nearly 80-year-old former top official as a "shock" move, heavily emphasizing the judge's rejection of the warrant. The structural takeaway here is profound: decades later, the trauma of 1992 remains an open wound, and current and former intelligence officials are realizing that the state's darkest secrets are still subject to aggressive, high-stakes prosecution. 

Moving from the courts to the markets, the digital banking revolution has collided head-on with Italian anti-mafia regulations. A Milan tribunal has placed the Italian branch of Revolut—the massively popular fintech app with 6 million local users—under judicial administration for a year. Prosecutors allege that Revolut’s famously frictionless onboarding process was essentially a sieve, allowing individuals tied to the 'Ndrangheta mafia, as well as hackers, to launder over 76 million euros. While La Repubblica highlights the irony of a tech giant trying to disrupt traditional finance only to be humbled by regulatory reality, La Stampa offers practical reassurance, noting that everyday customers' accounts and cards will continue to function normally while the bank's compliance systems are forcibly overhauled. 

Turning to the geopolitical front, Italy's controversial approach to migration is rapidly becoming the European standard. A coalition of five EU nations—including Germany, the Netherlands, and Austria—have announced a plan to open a "return hub" for rejected asylum seekers in Rwanda by 2027. The left-wing daily Il Manifesto vehemently condemns the move, describing it as the institutionalization of cruelty outsourced to Africa. However, center-right and government-aligned outlets are celebrating this as a historic paradigm shift. The real consequence is that the hardline strategy of externalizing borders—once pioneered by Rome with its centers in Albania—is now rewriting the entire European Union's asylum framework.

But before we go, a piece of good news from the capital. After 18 months of intensive restoration funded by the National Recovery Plan, Rome has opened a hidden jewel to the public for the first time: the Ulpia Library in the Forum of Trajan. Dating back to 112 AD, this underground marvel once held roughly ten thousand scrolls. It is a stunning reminder of the sheer scale of ancient Roman public infrastructure, now accessible through a newly built, illuminated walkway beneath the modern streets of the city.
