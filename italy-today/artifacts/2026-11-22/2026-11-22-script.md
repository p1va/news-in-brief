Turning to our main story, the nation is reeling from the suicide of Gianni De Gennaro, the former Chief of the Italian Police and a historic figure in the fight against the mafia. The 78-year-old was found dead in his Tuscan country home on Saturday, just days after learning he had been placed under investigation by prosecutors in Caltanissetta. The probe accused De Gennaro of orchestrating a cover-up to protect Cosa Nostra in the aftermath of the 1992 Via D'Amelio bombing, which killed judge Paolo Borsellino. Prosecutors alleged that De Gennaro was actively destroying documents and hiding evidence regarding the infamous missing "red agenda" of the murdered magistrate.

The media and political reaction has fractured into two deeply polarized camps, reflecting Italy's eternal war between the state and the judiciary. The center-right government has forcefully condemned the magistrates. Vice Premier Matteo Salvini and Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani, heavily amplified by conservative outlets like La Verità, have framed De Gennaro's death as the direct result of a "media-judicial massacre," arguing that loyal servants of the state are being tried and condemned in the press before ever seeing a courtroom. Conversely, the anti-establishment Il Fatto Quotidiano is maintaining focus on the gravity of the allegations, pointing out the dark shadows that have long hovered over the state's handling of the 1992 mafia massacres. 

The structural takeaway here is profound. De Gennaro’s tragic end exposes a justice system stretched to its absolute limits, where prosecutors are relying on decades-old memories, shifting testimonies, and historical theories to prosecute crimes from over thirty years ago. The ensuing clash is already prompting members of the ruling coalition to demand immediate reforms to curb the investigative powers of prosecutors, signaling a looming legislative battle over the soul of the Italian judicial system.

Moving from the courts to the streets, the government is responding to the violent student protests we covered yesterday with a harsh legislative crackdown. Following clashes that saw police pelted with paper bombs and electric scooters, Fratelli d'Italia has introduced a highly controversial amendment to the 2027 budget. The proposal would strip state university scholarships from any student formally investigated for participating in violent protests. Furthermore, it would authorize riot police to use colored tracking spray to physically mark demonstrators for later identification. Shifting to the economy, Il Sole 24 Ore has uncovered a hidden gem in the ongoing budget negotiations: the ghost of the Superbonus is still haunting the state's coffers. The government is currently weighing the expensive decision to scrap the so-called "spalmacrediti"—a controversial rule that forced construction companies and banks to spread their accumulated green-building tax credits over a ten-year period. Reversing this rule will cost the Treasury an estimated 550 million euros to cover the necessary government bonds. However, it is viewed as a desperate, necessary move to inject immediate liquidity into a construction sector that has been suffocated by the abrupt end of the pandemic-era subsidies, proving that the financial fallout from Italy's chaotic green transition is far from over.
