We begin with the Blitz.

In West Palm Beach, political violence has once again breached the perimeter of the American presidency. Less than 48 hours after the Supreme Court ruling against his tariff policies, a man armed with a rifle and a fuel canister was shot dead by the Secret Service while attempting to infiltrate Mar-a-Lago. *La Repubblica* notes that while President Trump was at the White House during the incident, the incursion highlights the extreme volatility of the American interior, with *Il Giornale* emphasizing the assailant’s "tactical stance" before being neutralized.

Closer to home, the Winter Olympics in Milan-Cortina have concluded with a historic flourish. Italy has secured 30 medals, a national record that shatters the previous benchmark set in 1994. *Corriere della Sera* reports that while the Netherlands narrowly pipped Italy in the final medal table, the institutional relief is palpable: Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni and President Mattarella have been awarded the Olympic Order in gold, a symbolic victory for a government that wagered heavily on the logistical success of these games.

However, the closing ceremony in Verona is tainted by a bizarre ethical scandal on the ice. The Israeli four-man bobsleigh team has been disqualified and withdrawn by its own national committee. *Fanpage* and *La Stampa* reveal the team attempted to subvert the rules by faking a medical illness for one athlete to swap in another illegally. It is a clumsy end to their campaign, cited by the Israeli committee as "conduct incompatible with Olympic values."

Diplomacy has taken a surreal turn in the North Atlantic. Following his failed bid to annex Greenland earlier this year, Donald Trump has announced he is sending a US Navy hospital ship to the island to "care for the unassisted sick." *Il Fatto Quotidiano* describes the Danish response as a mixture of bewilderment and irritation, with Copenhagen reminding Washington that Greenland has a fully functioning, free public healthcare system and does not require American charity, viewing the move as a transparent geopolitical provocation.

And finally, a weekend of tragedy for Italian families. In Civitanova Marche, the search for a 20-year-old man who brutally beat his mother on his birthday has ended with the discovery of his body in the harbor waters. Meanwhile, in Switzerland, *La Stampa* reports that the body of Luciano Capasso, missing for five days in Saint Moritz, has been found. The grief is compounded by fury: the family’s lawyer is accusing Swiss rescuers of negligence, claiming they knew his location via GPS for days but failed to act before the cold took him, telling the family with grim sarcasm to "prepare the funeral."

The lead story today is the total breakdown of the trans-Atlantic trade order, which has shifted from a diplomatic spat into a constitutional crisis with direct fallout for Italian industry.

Just one day after the US Supreme Court struck down President Trump’s initial tariff package as unconstitutional, the White House has effectively declared war on its own judiciary—and by extension, European exporters. Trump has invoked a different, older national security statute from 1974 to not only reinstate the duties but increase them to a flat 15 percent on all global imports. *Il Sole 24 Ore* describes the mood in Italian boardrooms as "panic," noting that companies are now facing a chaotic reality where American trade policy apparently supersedes American law.

The political reaction in Europe is fracturing along predictable lines. On the left, *La Repubblica* reports that France is pushing for a "bazooka" response, demanding immediate, retaliatory EU tariffs on American tech and bourbon. Conversely, the German Chancellor Friedrich Merz is urging caution, fearing a spiral that could sink the German manufacturing sector. *Il Giornale*, reflecting the Italian government's position, highlights a difficult tightrope walk for Rome: trying to maintain political affinity with the Trump administration while watching Italian unparalleled export sectors—from fashion to mechanics—being held hostage by Washington’s protectionist crusade. The takeaway is clear: the "rules-based order" that preserved Italian commerce is currently suspended.

Moving from the macro-economy to the granular details of a judicial investigation.

We are following up on yesterday's lead regarding the death of two-year-old Domenico at the Monaldi hospital in Naples. The investigation has rapidly intensified. The tragedy, caused by a donor heart that was "burned" by improper dry ice preservation, has seen the legal charge upgraded to manslaughter. *Il Mattino* and *AGI* report that the Carabinieri NAS have seized the mobile phones of six medical staff members. The investigation has moved beyond looking for a "system error" and is now hunting for individual culpability.

The narrative divide here is sharp. *Il Giornale* and *La Verità* are focusing on the "chain of command" failure between Bolzano, where the heart was harvested, and Naples, suggesting a lapse in basic medical competence. In contrast, *Fanpage* and the local press are amplifying the voice of Domenico’s mother, who is demanding to know why she was allegedly kept in the dark about the organ's condition while her son was on life support. The seizure of personal devices suggests prosecutors believe there may have been an attempt to coordinate a cover-up among the medical team after the transplant failed.

Turning to a significant development in defense that went largely unnoticed amidst the Olympic fanfare.

*Il Sole 24 Ore* reports that Italy has quietly joined a major strategic initiative with Germany, France, and the UK to develop "low-cost" autonomous drones. Dubbed the "LEAP" project, this is a direct response to the lessons learned from the war in Ukraine. The goal is to move away from multi-million dollar exquisite weapon systems and towards mass-producible, AI-driven defense assets. While *La Stampa* frames this as a necessary modernization of NATO capabilities, the significance lies in the industrial shift: this is the European defense sector acknowledging that in modern warfare, quantity has a quality all its own, and Italy’s aerospace industry is positioning itself to be a key manufacturer in this new, cheaper arms race.

But before we go, let's look to Assisi for a moment of quiet history.

For the first time in 800 years, the mortal remains of San Francesco are being exposed to the public. As *Corriere della Sera* details, the "Poverello’s" bones, usually sealed within the stone sarcophagus of the Basilica, have been placed in a plexiglass case for veneration until March 22nd. It is a stark, physical reminder of the man behind the myth. In a week dominated by trade wars, tragic deaths, and gold medals, the sight of a small, fragile skeleton that changed the course of Western spirituality serves as a grounding counterpoint—a reminder of endurance in its most humble form.
