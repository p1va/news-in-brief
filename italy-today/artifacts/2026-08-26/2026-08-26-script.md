The decision unlocks massive capital for the defense sector, but it has reignited a fierce ideological battle within the ruling coalition. 

The spin across the press highlights a deeply fractured majority. On the left, Il Fatto Quotidiano goes on the attack, framing this as a hypocritical moral failure. They point out that while the government claims it lacks funds for the crumbling public healthcare system, it magically fast-tracks 8 billion for weapons, while noting the icy silence from Matteo Salvini's Lega, which had opposed the move for months. Conversely, the centrist and pro-European press, like La Repubblica and La Stampa, frame this as a vital course correction. They applaud the move as Brussels promoting Rome's shift toward the Euro-Atlantic consensus, emphasizing that in a rapidly destabilizing world, Italy must modernize its military capabilities. 

The takeaway here is that Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni is prioritizing international credibility over domestic populist appeasement. By sidestepping the Lega’s hesitations and aligning firmly with NATO and EU defense strategies, she is signaling that Italy’s foreign policy is not up for coalition negotiation, even if it hands the opposition a potent talking point ahead of budget season.

Moving from international defense to domestic labor rights, the Milan prosecutor’s office has dropped a hammer on the gig economy. Prosecutor Paolo Storari issued a scathing rejection of food delivery giant Glovo’s request to exit judicial administration. Back in May, the platform promised to raise rider pay to 14 euros an hour to clear its name from labor exploitation charges. But according to the magistrates, those raises were a mirage. They found that Glovo's algorithm remains entirely opaque, meaning nearly 30 percent of the riders' labor goes uncompensated. It is a stark reminder that despite political promises to regulate the digital economy, algorithmic exploitation remains deeply entrenched in Italy's service sector.

Turning to a landmark moment for civil rights, the northern region of Lombardy has crossed a historic threshold. A 59-year-old man suffering from progressive multiple sclerosis became the first Italian to undergo assisted suicide entirely managed, equipped, and funded by a regional public health service. While the Constitutional Court legalized the practice under strict conditions years ago, patients have historically been forced into agonizing bureaucratic limbo. It took 290 days of legal battles by the Luca Coscioni Association to force the state’s hand, but this sets a monumental precedent: the Italian state has formally transitioned from merely tolerating assisted suicide to actively providing the medical infrastructure for it.
