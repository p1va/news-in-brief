Following up on our lead story from yesterday, the controversial "Melonellum" electoral law has passed its final hurdle. The Chamber of Deputies gave its definitive green light with 227 votes in favor and 164 against. The ruling coalition was holding its breath for this final vote because it was held by secret ballot—the ultimate ambush point for internal dissenters who might have feared losing their seats under the new preference voting rules. Instead, the right-wing majority held the line perfectly, officially archiving the old Rosatellum system and cementing a new proportional law with a majority premium, blocked party lists at the top, and the direct indication of the premier on the ballot.

The media framing of this legislative victory is predictably polarized. *Libero* and the conservative press echo Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni’s triumphant statements, framing the law as a return of power to the voters and a definitive proof that the center-right coalition is united and immune to parliamentary sabotage. Conversely, *La Repubblica* and *Il Fatto Quotidiano* cover the story as a democratic heist, highlighting the protests of the opposition who held up signs calling it a "scam law." *Il Manifesto* takes a darker view, warning that Meloni has just secured a "take-all" reform designed to mathematically lock in a right-wing government ahead of a rumored spring election. The ultimate takeaway is that the political maneuvering is over, and the battle now moves to the courts, with the opposition immediately announcing appeals to the Constitutional Court.

Moving from political engineering to the stark realities of the national ledger, Italy has received a bittersweet update from Brussels regarding its public spending. The European Union gave its initial approval for Italy to activate a national safeguard clause, granting roughly 29 billion euros in budget flexibility specifically dedicated to defense and energy costs. However, EU Economy Commissioner Valdis Dombrovskis immediately poured cold water on any further spending dreams. Speaking after the Eurogroup meeting, Dombrovskis explicitly stated that Europe will not introduce new forms of fiscal flexibility to help countries manage inflation. *Il Sole 24 Ore* notes the delicate balancing act for Economy Minister Giancarlo Giorgetti, who must now draft a national budget knowing that Brussels is watching every cent outside of defense and energy.

That fiscal strictness from Europe is colliding with a full-blown industrial crisis in the south of Italy. Acciaierie d’Italia has officially announced the immediate suspension of all production in the "hot area" of the former Ilva steelworks in Taranto, with ripple effects forcing shutdowns in Genoa and Novi Ligure. The company has requested massive furlough schemes for its workers, triggering an immediate backlash. Following a tense, three-hour meeting at Palazzo Chigi, national labor unions have called a 24-hour nationwide strike for the ex-Ilva workforce starting Monday. *Il Manifesto* calls it a "red alert" for the Italian economy, as the government scrambles to find a social safety net for thousands of workers in a steel sector that appears to be grinding to a complete halt.

But before we go, a moment of extraordinary medical innovation from Rome. At the Bambino Gesù Pediatric Hospital, surgeons have successfully saved a premature baby born weighing just one kilogram. The infant, suffering from a severe congenital heart defect, was far too small and fragile to survive open-heart surgery. Instead, in a groundbreaking procedure for an infant of this size, doctors used micro-catheters to insert a tiny metal stent and two microscopic flow regulators directly into the baby's heart, stabilizing the blood flow without ever opening the chest. The procedure bought the infant vital time to grow, and three months later, the baby has more than doubled in weight and is thriving. It is a remarkable reminder of the quiet, life-saving miracles happening in Italian hospitals every single day.
