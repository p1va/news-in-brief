Following up on yesterday's report, the terrifying incident in Modena—where thirty-one-year-old Salim El Koudri drove his car into a crowd of pedestrians—has completely fractured the political narrative in Rome. Today, investigators revealed that back in 2021, El Koudri sent violently anti-Christian emails to the local university. While he claims to have no memory of the emails and told his lawyer he merely "knew he was going to die that day," the right-wing press is seizing the moment. Outlets like Il Giornale and Libero, alongside Deputy Prime Minister Matteo Salvini, are weaponizing these emails as undeniable proof of Islamic radicalization, framing the attack as the inevitable result of failed integration and demanding immediate citizenship revocations. 

However, the progressive opposition is pushing back hard. La Repubblica and Il Fatto Quotidiano emphasize the defense lawyer's statements, which highlight El Koudri's documented schizoid personality disorder and isolation, explicitly ruling out any ties to terror networks. But the real friction is happening inside the government itself. Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani of Forza Italia made a highly publicized trip to Modena today not just to visit the victims, but to specifically thank the foreign-born citizens who bravely intervened to stop the attacker. 

The takeaway here is that the Meloni coalition is fundamentally divided on the concept of being Italian. Salvini’s sovereigntist faction is using the tragedy to push for punitive, hardline immigration and citizenship rollbacks, while Tajani’s moderate institutional right is actively defusing that rhetoric by highlighting the heroism of non-citizens. The government is struggling to project unity while fighting a proxy war over who actually belongs in the country.

Moving from domestic security to a brewing clash with Brussels, Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni has hit a brick wall. Yesterday, we reported that Meloni desperately wrote to European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen, asking that the EU Stability Pact exemptions—currently used for defense—be expanded to cover energy subsidies. Today, as reported by both Il Fatto Quotidiano and La Verità, the Commission's answer was a flat "no." With the Trump administration's global tariffs continuing to squeeze European exports, Rome is caught in a brutal trap. The government cannot subsidize the soaring energy costs for Italian manufacturing without breaking EU deficit rules, but adhering strictly to those rules threatens to slowly deindustrialize the peninsula. 

And speaking of long-term economic threats, a sleeper story in Il Sole 24 Ore today lays out the true, mathematical cost of Italy's demographic winter. A new banking association study warns that without immediate, structural intervention to boost the birth rate, the Italian GDP will collapse by a staggering eighteen percent by 2050. It is a stark reminder that beneath the daily political noise, the country is facing an existential math problem: a shrinking pool of working-age citizens cannot sustain economic growth or the current pension system.

But before we go, let us look at a booming sector of the Italian lifestyle that ties directly into that demographic shift. While human birth rates plummet, pet ownership is skyrocketing. According to Il Sole 24 Ore, Italians traveling with their dogs and cats has evolved from a niche habit into an absolute economic powerhouse. The "pet-friendly vacation" market is now worth nearly nine and a half billion euros annually and is projected to double in the coming years. So, while the national nurseries may be emptying out, the beachside dog resorts are fully booked.
