Turning our attention to domestic politics, the Meloni government has just picked a fight with one of the most powerful and protected institutions in the country: the judiciary. Deep inside the 157 articles of the 2027 Budget Law lies a seemingly bureaucratic tweak with massive financial and political implications. Through Article 67, the government is changing how salary increases for magistrates are calculated. By reintroducing a "weighted average" and explicitly tying their pay bumps to the broader, often slower-growing pool of regular public sector employees, the state is effectively capping judicial salaries and eliminating back-pay recalculations. 

The media framing of this measure is sharply divided. The financial daily Il Sole 24 Ore takes a clinical view, calling it a necessary "double brake" on runaway public spending. They treat it as a rational accounting measure to restore limits to the global state budget. Conversely, progressive and institutional outlets like La Repubblica highlight the immediate pushback, warning of a looming institutional clash as the National Association of Magistrates raises the alarm. The ultimate takeaway here is a continuation of the narrative we discussed yesterday. The Italian state is desperately cash-strapped. Just as it squeezed pensioners and capped public school hiring to meet European debt targets, it is now targeting the historical privileges of the judiciary, framing the cuts as an act of national fairness.

Moving from the courts to the halls of power, Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni has officially mapped out her political endgame. In an anticipation of a new book by journalist Bruno Vespa, Meloni explicitly ruled out a future run for the Quirinale—the Presidency of the Republic—stating she is entirely focused on securing a second mandate as Prime Minister. La Stampa and La Repubblica focus heavily on her aggressive tone, as she lambasted the opposition for what she called a "cultural racism" that assumes only left-wing figures are fit to be Head of State. By taking her name out of the hat for the Presidency, Meloni is consolidating her grip on the executive branch and challenging the opposition to defeat her at the ballot box, not in backroom parliamentary deals.

Meanwhile, scanning the fine print of that same Budget Law reveals a quiet surrender on global trade. Adnkronos and Il Sole 24 Ore report that the government has officially scrapped a planned two-euro tax on small, non-EU packages. Originally designed to curb the endless flood of cheap e-commerce shipments from China, the measure has been aborted before it even took effect. While the state loses an estimated 91 million euros a year in projected revenue, the government ultimately decided that risking a consumer backlash over the cost of cheap online goods was a political price too high to pay during a cost-of-living crisis.
