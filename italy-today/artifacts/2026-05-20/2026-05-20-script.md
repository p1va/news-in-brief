The political spin in Italy is incredibly polarized. Left-leaning and anti-establishment outlets like *L'Unità* and *Fanpage* are furious, accusing the Meloni government of staggering hypocrisy. They argue that Rome is merely pretending to be outraged for the cameras while effectively abandoning Italian citizens to the abuses of the Israeli military. On the opposite side, conservative papers like *Il Giornale* and *Libero* are taking a hardline stance, framing the activists as deliberate provocateurs and defending Israel's absolute right to intercept the ships, with *Libero* publishing op-eds demanding that "terrorist sympathizers" face the consequences of their actions.

The takeaway here is that the Meloni government has been backed into a diplomatic corner. The footage was so inflammatory that Prime Minister Meloni and Foreign Minister Antonio Tajani were forced to officially summon the Israeli ambassador, labeling the treatment of the detainees "unacceptable" and demanding their immediate release. Rome is currently trying to balance its strong institutional alliance with Israel against the severe domestic political cost of allowing a foreign allied minister to publicly humiliate Italian citizens.

Moving from international diplomacy to domestic justice, the executive branch has suffered a significant legal blow. A Rome appeals court has confirmed an eight-month prison sentence for Justice Undersecretary Andrea Delmastro, a high-ranking member of Meloni’s Brothers of Italy party. The conviction stems from the 2023 "Cospito case," where Delmastro was found guilty of revealing classified state wiretaps to smear political opponents in Parliament. *La Repubblica* treats the ruling as a massive institutional embarrassment, highlighting that a man convicted of leaking state secrets continues to hold a top position in the Justice Ministry. Conversely, the conservative *Il Giornale* downplays the verdict as a minor procedural hiccup, emphasizing that Delmastro will appeal to the Supreme Court. Regardless of the spin, the conviction guarantees that the grueling, structural cold war between Meloni’s executive branch and the Italian judiciary will continue to escalate.

Turning to the economy, the government has officially passed its new Fiscal Decree, once again utilizing a confidence vote to shut down parliamentary debate. *Il Sole 24 Ore* focuses on the mechanics of the law, noting that it extends deadlines for businesses to reach tax settlements and opens a new amnesty window for unpaid local fines and regional taxes. However, *Il Fatto Quotidiano* attacks the decree ruthlessly, framing it as an "omnibus" text utterly devoid of structural reform. They argue it is simply another rolling amnesty designed to reward tax evaders and plug immediate holes in the state budget without solving Italy's chronic revenue crisis.
