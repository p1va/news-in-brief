Let's start with a rapid sweep of today's top stories. Following up on yesterday's Revolut security breach, the hackers have upped the ante, now claiming to possess sensitive data from the Italian Police and demanding a three million dollar ransom. On the macro front, Istat confirms that inflation ticked up to 3.3 percent in August, driven entirely by surging energy costs. Across the Atlantic, Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh directly defied President Donald Trump today, hiking US interest rates by a quarter point to combat sticky inflation. In Brussels, Commission President Ursula von der Leyen proposed an unprecedented move to make Canada the European Union's first "associate member," a direct effort to consolidate Western defense in an increasingly fractured world. Domestically, there is a brief reprieve on the trade front; Il Sole 24 Ore reports the government has delayed the new three-euro tariff on non-EU micro-packages until November. Finally, Premier Giorgia Meloni has pulled a rabbit out of her hat, announcing the suspension of one of the country's most universally despised taxes.

That surprise announcement is a massive cut to the "bollo auto," Italy's annual vehicle ownership tax. The Council of Ministers has officially approved a decree abolishing the tax for 2027 on all motorcycles and small-to-medium cars under 80 kilowatts. This move will affect roughly 14 million Italians and cost the state an estimated 2.5 billion euros, which the government claims will be offset using savings from the post-pandemic National Recovery and Resilience Plan.

The framing of this tax cut is highly polarized. The center-right press, led by Libero and Il Giornale, is celebrating the decree as a massive victory for the working class. They amplify Premier Meloni's narrative that while the opposition talks about wealth taxes, the government is delivering structural relief to everyday citizens who need their vehicles to commute. Conversely, the opposition media is crying foul. Il Fatto Quotidiano aggressively dismisses the move as a desperate "electoral bribe," comparing it to the populist promises of the late Silvio Berlusconi. Left-leaning La Repubblica questions the financial sustainability of the maneuver, pointing out that the cut is currently only funded for a single year and will blow a massive hole in the budgets of regional governments that rely on the bollo for revenue. 

The ultimate takeaway here is that the government is aggressively front-loading its political capital. By redirecting European recovery funds into immediate, highly visible tax relief, the ruling coalition is cementing its base and daring the opposition—and Brussels—to object to putting money directly back into the pockets of Italian drivers.

Moving from the cabinet to the courts, a structural shift in Italy's balance of power has just been finalized. The Superior Council of the Magistracy has officially greenlit the implementation of psycho-aptitude tests for all incoming judges and prosecutors. The new tests will evaluate candidates on emotional stability, stress management, and empathy. While the government argues this ensures the mental fitness of those holding the power to strip away a citizen's freedom, the National Association of Magistrates is outraged. They view the tests as a retaliatory tool designed by the political class to intimidate, condition, and ultimately weaken the independence of the judiciary.

Turning to a darker reality of the Italian social fabric, the national statistics agency Istat released a sobering report today on violence against women. Moving past the daily noise of episodic street crime, the data reveals a profound systemic failure: nearly 15 percent of all Italian women—roughly three million citizens—suffered physical or sexual violence before the age of 16. Most disturbingly, the report highlights that the vast majority of these abuses occurred not in dark alleys by strangers, but within the home, perpetrated by parents, relatives, or close family friends.

But before we go, we bid farewell to a living piece of Italian history. Vitantonio Lovallo, officially Italy's oldest man, passed away today at the age of 112 in his small village in the southern region of Basilicata. Known affectionately as "Zitòn," Lovallo lived a life that spanned two world wars, surviving a German prison camp before returning home to work his land as a shepherd and farmer. According to his family, his secret was a simple rural diet and unyielding resilience—so much so that he was still actively working the fields at 109 years old.
