But dominating the national conversation this weekend is the Constitutional Justice Referendum. Tomorrow morning, polling stations will open across the country, asking Italians to vote on a structural overhaul of the judicial system, most notably the strict separation of careers between judges and prosecutors. Because this is a constitutional referendum, no minimum turnout quorum is required, meaning the result will stand regardless of how many people show up. 

However, what was supposed to be a dry, technical vote on legal architecture has mutated into a bitter political proxy war, fueled by the ongoing scandal surrounding Justice Undersecretary Andrea Delmastro. Following up on yesterday's revelations, new photographs have surfaced showing Delmastro dining with individuals tied to the Senese crime syndicate. Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni continues to shield her undersecretary, dismissing the photos as mere "lightness." As highlighted by conservative outlets like Il Giornale and La Verità, the government is leaning heavily into the narrative of a "little hand"—a shadow conspiracy by rogue, left-wing magistrates deliberately timing these leaks to derail tomorrow's vote. 

Conversely, the progressive opposition is weaponizing the scandal. La Repubblica and Fanpage are hammering the government, arguing that Delmastro's refusal to resign proves the ruling coalition is fundamentally unfit to reform the justice system. For the center-left, the referendum is not about efficiency, but about neutering the independence of the very prosecutors who investigate political corruption. The ultimate takeaway is that tomorrow's vote will serve as a definitive stress test: a verdict not just on how trials are conducted, but on who truly wields power in Italy—the executive branch or the judiciary.

Moving from the courts to heavy industry, a massive development has quietly unfolded regarding Italy's most troubled manufacturing asset. According to Il Sole 24 Ore, the Indian multinational Jindal Group has officially submitted a binding offer to the government to acquire the entirety of the ex-Ilva steelworks in Taranto. The plant, once the largest steel mill in Europe, has been trapped in a decade-long purgatory of environmental scandals, state bailouts, and failed foreign takeovers. If the Meloni government accepts this bid, it could finally inject the private capital needed to keep the blast furnaces burning, though unions are already demanding to see the hidden industrial plan to ensure mass layoffs are not part of the deal.

Turning to the digital economy, the government is about to fundamentally change how you choose your next restaurant or hotel. Buried in a new legislative package aimed at protecting small and medium enterprises, Rome is cracking down on the multi-million euro black market of fake online reviews. Platforms like TripAdvisor and The Fork will now be legally required to verify reviews by linking them to an actual digital receipt, which must be posted within thirty days of the purchase. While tech companies will likely push back on the administrative burden, Il Sole 24 Ore notes this regulatory shift aims to clean up the digital high street, protecting legitimate businesses from coordinated extortion and smear campaigns.
