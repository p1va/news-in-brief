The most consequential story of the day, however, comes from the courts in Milan, where a judicial decree threatens to shut down the largest steelworks in Europe. The Milan Court of Appeals has ordered the former Ilva steel plant in Taranto to completely shut down its "hot area" within 90 days. The judges ruled that the plant cannot operate until it completely removes all remaining asbestos and drastically reduces its toxic dust emissions. The ruling immediately prompted Prime Minister Meloni to convene an emergency cabinet meeting at Palazzo Chigi this evening. 

The national press is treating this as an absolute earthquake, though the fault lines depend on who you read. Progressive and anti-establishment outlets like *Il Fatto Quotidiano* and *La Stampa* are championing the ruling as a historic victory for the "Genitori Tarantini"—a local parents' committee—framing it as the moment the state was finally forced to prioritize human lives and climate justice over corporate profits. Conversely, *Il Sole 24 Ore* and government sources view this as a bureaucratic and industrial nightmare. The state just allocated another 100 million euros to keep the plant's special administration afloat, and halting the hot area effectively kills the factory's production capacity. 

The takeaway here is that the eternal, tragic compromise in Taranto—choosing between the right to health and the right to work—has hit a legal wall. At a time when the global economy is bracing for the impact of heavy American trade tariffs, shutting down Italy's primary domestic steel producer could cripple the nation's broader manufacturing supply chain, placing the Meloni government in an incredibly precarious position.

Moving from industrial crises to infrastructure battles, the government's flagship megaproject is facing a severe legal challenge. The CGIL, Italy's largest trade union, has filed a formal complaint with the Court of Auditors, alleging a 1.3 billion euro waste of public funds regarding the Strait of Messina Bridge. As highlighted by *La Repubblica*, the union argues that these funds are being illegally drained from Sicily's development and cohesion budget to finance preliminary contracts, even though the final executive project for the bridge has not yet been officially approved. The infrastructure ministry defends the spending as necessary preparatory work, but this legal maneuver attempts to corner the government, turning a highly polarized political debate into a potential judicial quagmire for state accountants.

Meanwhile, looking at a quiet but massive shift for consumers, a major regulatory change is arriving at the end of the week. As reported by ANSA, starting July 31st, Italy will officially enforce the European Union's new "Right to Repair" directive. Manufacturers of household appliances, smartphones, and electronics will now be legally obligated to provide spare parts and affordable repair services to consumers, actively preventing the premature disposal of goods. While it lacks the screaming headlines of political scandals, this directive fundamentally rewrites the retail economy, striking a structural blow against planned obsolescence and changing how Italians will interact with the technology they buy.

With millions of Italians heading out for the summer holidays, the Consortium for the Protection of Buffalo Mozzarella has released a humorous official guide on the five correct postures for eating their famous cheese. The guide ranges from the "Pitoffio"—a Neapolitan term for standing with your legs apart and leaning forward so the milk doesn't ruin your shirt—to the "Totò," which involves squeezing the mozzarella between your thumb and index finger to test the milk flow before taking a bite, just like in the classic film *Miseria e Nobiltà*. It is a gentle reminder that in Italy, even the simplest meal comes with its own deeply rooted choreography.
