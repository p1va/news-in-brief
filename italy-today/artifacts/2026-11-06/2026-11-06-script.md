But the main story today is a spectacular institutional trainwreck that played out over just twenty-four hours, exposing the fragile mechanics of the ruling coalition’s appointments. In a highly controversial move yesterday, the Meloni government nominated Trifone "Nuccio" Altieri to be the new president of ANAC, Italy’s National Anti-Corruption Authority. The problem? Altieri is a former parliamentarian who served as a department head for the Lega party just two years ago. Italian law strictly mandates a three-year "cooling-off" period between holding a political office and leading an independent guarantor institution. 

The political fallout was instantaneous, and the Italian press is painting two very different pictures of the debacle. Progressive outlets like La Repubblica and Il Fatto Quotidiano immediately framed the nomination as a dangerous institutional hijack. They pointed out that ANAC has recently been deeply critical of the procurement processes for the Strait of Messina Bridge—Matteo Salvini’s flagship infrastructure project. Installing a recent Lega operative to oversee the agency auditing the Lega’s largest project was, according to the opposition, a brazen conflict of interest. 

On the other side of the spectrum, the government initially tried to tough it out. As reported by Il Sole 24 Ore, Prime Minister Meloni defended the choice as completely within the cabinet's rights, while Lega leader Matteo Salvini publicly hoped the paperwork was "regular." However, the sheer weight of the statutory violation proved impossible to spin. By late evening, Altieri was forced to formally withdraw his candidacy to save the executive from further embarrassment. The takeaway is clear: while the government commands a solid majority, its attempts to place loyalists into independent oversight roles are facing intense, and in this case successful, pushback from the country's institutional guardrails.

Moving from the halls of power to the streets, a quiet revolution is coming to Italian drivers. According to Il Messaggero and Libero, the upcoming reform of the Highway Code features a sweeping digitalization mandate that will fundamentally change how citizens interact with law enforcement. Starting exactly on January 1, 2028, the legal obligation to carry physical car documents—including your driver's license, your vehicle registration, and your insurance certificate—will be completely abolished on national territory. Instead, traffic police will verify everything remotely through a centralized database linked to the national vehicle registry. While this aims to eliminate bureaucratic headaches and fines for forgetting a wallet at home, it represents a massive logistical challenge for the state's digital infrastructure, which must now guarantee zero downtime during roadside checks. 

Turning to the industrial sector, a sleeper story of immense economic consequence has just emerged from the south. The Adnkronos news agency reports that the "hot area" of the Ex-Ilva steelworks in Taranto—the largest steel plant in Europe—has been completely shut down. The plant's management executed the shutdown in direct compliance with a ruling from the Milan Court of Appeal. This sudden halt in the blast furnaces is a devastating blow to Italy's manufacturing output and signals a critical escalation in the decades-long legal and environmental battle over the facility's future, threatening thousands of jobs and the nation's industrial sovereignty.

But before we go, an innovative solution to a uniquely Italian problem is gaining traction. With small southern villages facing existential demographic collapse, the Calabria region has launched the "Ripopola Calabria" plan. As detailed by Il Sole 24 Ore, the regional government is offering public housing completely rent-free for ten years to anyone willing to move their legal residence to one of these emptying towns. The catch? You have to actually live there. To sweeten the deal, the regional government is even paying for the structural renovations. It is a desperate, yet highly pragmatic attempt to turn abandoned real estate into a lifeline for Italy's disappearing rural communities.
