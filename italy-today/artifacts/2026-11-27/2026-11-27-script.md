The domestic reaction to the tragedy is sharply divided, exposing a deep rift over Italy's role in the global arms trade. *La Verità* and the conservative press frame this strictly as an unprovoked act of aggression by Iranian proxies, emphasizing the need for Italy to maintain a firm geopolitical stance alongside its Western allies. 

Conversely, the progressive and left-wing press are treating this primarily as a labor and moral crisis. *Il Manifesto* ran a scathing front page calling it the "War on the Ring Road," a nod to the fact that these global defense contracts are fulfilled by regular blue-collar workers based in Rome's suburbs. Meanwhile, *La Repubblica* amplifies the demands of the FIOM metalworkers' union, which is now demanding the immediate repatriation of all Italian civilian defense contractors currently deployed in active war zones. The takeaway is that as global conflicts widen, the line between Italy’s corporate defense exports and active frontline engagement is blurring, turning civilian workers into geopolitical casualties.

Moving from foreign conflicts to domestic economic battles, the government's 2027 budget law is sparking a fierce class war over taxation. The center-right majority is pushing forward with an expansion of the 15 percent "flat tax" for self-employed workers and freelancers, raising the revenue cap to 100,000 euros. 

*Il Fatto Quotidiano* and *Il Manifesto* are blasting the maneuver as an inequitable, four-billion-euro gift to the wealthy. They cite union data showing that a precarious salaried worker could end up paying up to 18,000 euros more in taxes per year than a self-employed professional earning the exact same gross amount. Defending the move in the pages of *Il Sole 24 Ore*, Prime Minister Meloni accuses the left of basic financial illiteracy, arguing they are deliberately confusing gross business revenue—which must cover operating costs—with a salaried worker's net take-home pay. Ultimately, the budget exposes a structural divide in the Italian workforce, as the government heavily incentivizes independent contracting over traditional employment.

Returning to a story that continues to shake the highest levels of the Italian state, the final letters of former police chief Gianni De Gennaro have been published following his suicide last weekend. In his eight-page testament, De Gennaro wrote that "a condemned man cannot lie," insisting he never betrayed the state, but cryptically added that he "obeyed orders" and fulfilled tasks "even against his will." 

The institutional optics surrounding his death are incredibly tense. *Il Manifesto* notes that President Sergio Mattarella is making a profound, silent statement of state continuity by choosing to personally attend De Gennaro's funeral on Monday. In stark contrast, Prime Minister Meloni is pointedly keeping her distance. She told reporters she is unsure if she will attend, warning against the "games of fan clubs" and insisting that the priority is to help prosecutors clear the fog surrounding the mafia massacres of the 1990s. *La Stampa* suggests the real answers might lie in the encrypted hard drives De Gennaro left behind, which he explicitly hid from the magistrates he despised.
