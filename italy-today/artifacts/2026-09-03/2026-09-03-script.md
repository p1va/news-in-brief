The Meloni government is officially reaching 1,413 days in power, matching and surpassing the second Berlusconi administration to become the longest-lasting government in the history of the Italian Republic. Fratelli d'Italia is pulling out all the stops for a grand celebration in Puglia, framing this unprecedented longevity as the ultimate proof of their governing mandate. 

However, the political spin surrounding this anniversary is heavily polarized. The institutional and conservative press, such as Il Sole 24 Ore, treats the milestone as a launchpad, noting that Meloni is using this newfound stability to prepare structural moves on taxes and national security, boasting of 1.3 million new jobs created during her tenure. But across the aisle, the opposition press is painting a very different picture. La Repubblica and Il Manifesto have published aggressive fact-checking reports, arguing that the true price of this political longevity is a surge in the "working poor" and a total failure in migration management. The takeaway here is clear: in Italy's notoriously volatile political system, mere survival is being weaponized as a historic victory by the right, while the left argues that institutional stability means very little if it fails to translate into improved living conditions for the working class.

More explosively, progressive outlets like La Stampa note that the shadow of the Scelba Law—which forbids the reconstitution of the fascist party—hangs over the inquiry. While Vannacci’s legal team aggressively denies any wrongdoing, calling the investigation a desperate witch hunt, the probe adds legal pressure to the political friction already burning between Vannacci’s populist faction and the moderate wing of the governing coalition.

The sudden spike is forcing the government's hand. Rome is now rushing to draft a mini-extension of the excise duty cuts until September 10th to ease the burden on transport logistics and lower-income families. Consumer associations are warning that these targeted, temporary discounts are merely a band-aid on a gaping wound, cautioning that if fuel costs aren't stabilized soon, the cascading effect on grocery prices will wipe out whatever modest wage gains Italian workers have seen this year.
