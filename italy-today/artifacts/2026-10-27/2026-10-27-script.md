But our main focus tonight is the government's highly anticipated 2027 budget law, which is heading to the Council of Ministers tomorrow, and the massive financial maneuvers happening in its shadow. Finance Minister Giancarlo Giorgetti is attempting to close a 23.5 billion euro package that relies heavily on a delicate balancing act between populist promises and European fiscal reality. The headline measures include a new 5 percent flat tax for new hires under 35, the detaxation of the thirteenth-month salary, and the controversial return of the "Quota 100" early retirement scheme. 

The media synthesis of this budget is sharply divided. La Verità and the conservative press are celebrating the maneuver as a victory for youth employment and pensioners, praising the government for keeping its electoral promises while navigating tight European constraints. On the opposite end, Il Fatto Quotidiano is declaring the death of the middle class, blasting the government for abandoning promised income tax cuts in favor of targeted demographic bonuses. But the real friction lies in how the state plans to pay for it all. According to Il Sole 24 Ore, the government is quietly preparing a new, aggressive "Tobin tax" on stock purchases, aiming to extract roughly 5 billion euros directly from the financial sector. This aggressive tax grab comes on the exact same day that the European Central Bank officially greenlit Intesa Sanpaolo’s historic acquisition of a controlling stake in Monte dei Paschi di Siena. In essence, the government is moving to heavily tax the very banking champions it has spent the last year helping to consolidate.

To combat this, the new code introduces a mathematically blunt instrument: from now on, if you attempt to register a 21st vehicle under your name, you must officially prove an annual income of over 50,000 euros. It is a strangely specific, highly effective bureaucratic trap designed to instantly flag phantom fleets.
