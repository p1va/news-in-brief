Moving to our main story, the Italian classroom has just become the ultimate legislative battlefield. Today, the Senate gave final approval to Education Minister Giuseppe Valditara's highly contested "informed consent" bill. The new law makes it strictly mandatory for schools to obtain written parental permission before conducting any extracurricular activities or lessons related to sexuality and affectivity. Furthermore, it completely bans any form of sex education in elementary and middle schools. 

The institutional right is treating this as a monumental cultural victory. Outlets like Il Giornale and Libero are quoting Minister Valditara, who frames the law as a necessary shield to "protect children from gender propaganda" and a restoration of constitutional parental rights over state overreach. However, the opposition's reaction is furious. La Repubblica and Il Fatto Quotidiano are blasting the law as "medieval obscurantism," heavily featuring quotes from Democratic Party and civil rights leaders who argue that in a country struggling with a crisis of gender-based violence, restricting sex education to a parental opt-in system removes a vital tool for prevention and awareness. 

The takeaway here is that the government is aggressively fulfilling its social-conservative mandate, not just through rhetoric, but by hardwiring its cultural values into the state apparatus. By subordinating education on sexuality to a parental veto, the state is fundamentally altering its role in the social development of the next generation.

Turning from the culture wars to the courts, a thirty-year judicial saga has reached what appears to be its final chapter. The judge for preliminary investigations in Florence has officially archived the probe into the late Silvio Berlusconi and his right-hand man Marcello Dell'Utri regarding their alleged role as hidden masterminds behind the Mafia bombings of 1993. The judge ruled that after three decades of investigations, there are simply no concrete elements proving direct contacts between Cosa Nostra and the former Prime Minister regarding the terror campaign. The conservative press, including Il Giornale, is running victory laps, quoting Prime Minister Giorgia Meloni who stated that this archives "thirty years of infamous suspicion" against the center-right. Left-leaning papers like Il Fatto Quotidiano, which has built its identity on these exact prosecutorial theories, begrudgingly acknowledge the dismissal but maintain that the archiving only clears criminal liability, not the historical shadows surrounding the origins of Forza Italia. Regardless of the spin, this ruling formally closes one of the most explosive and divisive judicial theories in modern Italian history.

Meanwhile, in a quiet but massive shift for the country's industrial future, the Lower House has approved a framework bill that paves the way for Italy's return to nuclear energy. Decades after a national referendum banned nuclear power, the new legislation mandates the government to establish a regulatory framework for small modular reactors. Il Sole 24 Ore highlights the economic imperative behind the vote, framing it as a desperate necessity to secure Italy's energy independence in an era of harsh global tariffs and geopolitical instability. The Green-Left alliance vehemently protested the vote, calling it a subversion of democracy, but the structural reality is that the government believes the country's manufacturing base can no longer survive on imported fossil fuels alone. The bill now heads to the Senate.

Across the Italian press today, from La Stampa to Il Giornale, tributes are pouring in for a woman whose work managed to translate the trauma of the Iranian revolution and the universal quest for freedom into a masterpiece that resonated across the entire world.
