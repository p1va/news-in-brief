Our main story tonight focuses on the winter ahead and the cost of keeping the lights on. After months of grueling negotiations, the Italian government announced today that it has reached a preliminary agreement with the European Commission on the "Dl Bollette"—a vital decree designed to cap the price of gas and shield Italian families and businesses from surging electricity costs. 

The spin across the media highlights the delicate tightrope Rome is walking with Brussels. The right-wing press, led by Libero and La Verità, is taking a victory lap. They frame this as a massive win for Prime Minister Meloni, portraying her as a tenacious leader who wrestled concessions out of Europe to ensure Italians won't freeze this winter. Conversely, the opposition press is pouring cold water on the celebration. Il Fatto Quotidiano and La Repubblica are quick to point out the fine print: the EU forced Italy to exclude the carbon tax costs—known as the ETS system—from the relief package. They highlight European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen's rather blunt statement today that while they reached a deal, Brussels "will not perform miracles."

The takeaway here is that while Meloni gains immediate political capital by promising lower utility bills in the short term, the strict limitations of the European State Aid framework remain firmly in place. The government successfully negotiated a temporary safety net, but by leaving the carbon tax costs entirely on the shoulders of the consumer, the underlying vulnerability of Italy's energy sector to international market shocks is far from solved.

Speaking of the state budget and the hunt for cash we discussed yesterday, a massive institutional payout is quietly making waves. While Finance Minister Giancarlo Giorgetti spent the day defending the government's controversial 5-billion-euro levy on banks, Il Sole 24 Ore revealed a much quieter line item in the 2027 budget document sent to Brussels. Buried in a 1.9-billion-euro "sentence fund" is a massive payout directed entirely at Italian magistrates. Thanks to a recent Council of State ruling on pay scales, the state's 11,000 judges are in line to receive up to 100,000 euros each in salary bumps and back pay. It is a striking juxtaposition: a populist government aggressively taxing the financial sector to balance the books, while legally forced to hand out six-figure retroactive bonuses to the judicial establishment.

And if you want to know why the government is always scraping the barrel for tax revenue, the national statistics agency, ISTAT, provided the answer today. According to their latest report, Italy's shadow and illegal economy ballooned to 218 billion euros in 2024. That means practically 10 percent of the entire Italian GDP is entirely off the books. The data reveals that while violent organized crime is at historic lows, white-collar evasion is thriving, fueled by an army of 3.2 million irregular, off-the-books workers.

It is a gentle reminder that sometimes, the best parts of Italy are the ones furthest from the noise.
