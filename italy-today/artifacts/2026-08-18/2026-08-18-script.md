Following Germany's lead, Switzerland has officially announced it will resume transferring asylum seekers back to Italy starting in late August. These are the so-called "Dublin cases"—migrants who first landed in Italy but crossed the Alps to claim asylum elsewhere. After a nearly four-year freeze, Bern has already booked the first flights to send them back.

The media framing of this development reveals a stark divide. Right-wing papers like La Verità and Libero are covering this with alarm, framing the move as proof that Northern Europe is callously dumping migrants back onto Italian shores. They use this to fuel the sovereigntist demand for harder external borders and a crackdown on NGOs. On the other side, progressive and centrist dailies like Il Fatto Quotidiano and La Stampa treat the Swiss decision clinically, viewing it simply as the functional reality of the newly enforced European migration pact and the established Dublin regulation. 

The takeaway here is highly consequential for the Meloni government. For years, the domestic political promise has been to stop the influx of migrants from the Mediterranean. But as neighboring countries enforce strict return policies, Italy is finding itself physically sandwiched—acting as the permanent waiting room for a continent that is quietly closing its internal doors.

Moving from our borders to the beating heart of Italian heavy industry, there is a massive development regarding the ex-Ilva steelworks in Taranto. Federacciai, the federation of Italian steelmakers, alongside 14 associated national companies, has officially presented a manifestation of interest to take over the struggling plant. As La Stampa highlights, this opens the door to a fully Italian "cordata"—or consortium—aiming to rescue the strategic asset and keep it out of the hands of foreign conglomerates like the Indian group Jindal. It is a critical test for the government's industrial sovereignty agenda, determining whether domestic private capital can finally fix a decades-long environmental and economic disaster.
