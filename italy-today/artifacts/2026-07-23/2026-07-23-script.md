The Meloni cabinet has officially greenlit what the press is dubbing the "Anti-Maranza" decree—a colloquial term used to describe unruly youth street gangs. The new law fundamentally alters how minors aged 14 to 18 are prosecuted in Italy. Currently, prosecutors must prove a minor is fully capable of understanding their actions. The new decree inverts this burden of proof: teenagers are now presumed legally responsible and imputable by default, unless their defense can explicitly prove otherwise.

The narrative split in the press is absolute. The right-wing media, spearheaded by Libero and Il Giornale, is cheering the crackdown. They frame the law as a necessary shield against organized crime syndicates that exploit the perceived untouchability of teenagers to traffic drugs and commit violent robberies. 

Conversely, the opposition press is furious. La Repubblica and Il Fatto Quotidiano label the decree a perverse mechanism of pure repression. They argue the government is abandoning rehabilitation in favor of incarceration, masking its failure to invest in schools and marginalized suburbs with harsh, headline-grabbing penalties. The ultimate takeaway is clear: the Meloni government is aggressively cementing its "law and order" brand, continuing a structural pattern of using the penal code to solve complex, systemic social issues.

With the national parliament completely paralyzed on the issue due to deep ethical divisions within the ruling right-wing coalition, progressive regional governments are stepping into the legislative void to implement framework rulings previously handed down by the Constitutional Court.
