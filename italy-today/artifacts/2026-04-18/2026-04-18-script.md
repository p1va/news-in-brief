Water cannons were deployed as thousands marched against immigration and European Union policies. However, the true significance of the rally wasn't the street violence; it was Salvini's explicit demand from the stage. The Deputy Prime Minister called for the immediate suspension of the EU Green Deal and a return to purchasing Russian gas and oil.

The domestic press is sharply divided on this pivot. The progressive opposition, led by La Repubblica and Fanpage, paints the rally as a xenophobic echo chamber, highlighting the anti-Muslim chants and framing Salvini's overture to Moscow as a dangerous betrayal of Western alliances. On the flip side, the conservative Il Giornale and Libero defend the event as a pragmatic shield for the Italian working class. They argue that with Trump's sweeping tariffs strangling European exports and the Hormuz blockade triggering a massive spike in fuel costs, Brussels' climate austerity is economic suicide, and tapping back into Russian energy is a matter of sheer national survival. The ultimate takeaway here is a widening crack within the Meloni government itself. While the Prime Minister is in Paris trying to build an independent European military coalition to secure the Strait of Hormuz, her own Deputy Prime Minister is in Milan telling voters the solution isn't a European navy, but a deal with Vladimir Putin. 

Moving from the streets to the halls of Parliament, the government's sweeping Security Decree is facing a fierce backlash from an unexpected corner: the legal profession. An amendment quietly inserted into the decree offers a 615-euro bonus to lawyers who convince their migrant clients to accept "assisted voluntary repatriation." The progressive Il Fatto Quotidiano calls it a bounty system designed to buy off public defenders, while the economic daily Il Sole 24 Ore reports that the National Forensic Council is furious, calling the measure an unconstitutional conflict of interest. Further inflaming the opposition, Il Sole 24 Ore uncovered another sleeper amendment in the bill that greenlights American ICE-style crowd control weapons for Italian police, including programmable deformation bullets and ultraviolet markers for tracking protesters. 

On a much quieter, but deeply consequential bureaucratic note, millions of Italians are on the clock. August 3rd marks the hard deadline for the death of the Italian paper identity card. Driven by a new EU regulation aimed at standardizing continental security and preventing forgery, citizens still holding the old, dog-eared paper booklets have less than four months to upgrade to the electronic version. Those who fail to do so will lose access to essential digital government services and face severe restrictions at European border crossings.
